Slide 1: Knee joint: the moving parts

The knee joins the femur (thigh bone) to the tibia (main shin bone), while the patella (kneecap) slides over the front of the femur. Smooth cartilage covers the contacting surfaces; ligaments join bone to bone and steady the joint. The fibula (slender outer lower-leg bone) is a nearby attachment landmark, rather than a surface of the main knee joint.

Takeaway: Locate femur, tibia and patella before learning the soft tissues.

Key terms

Joint — A meeting between bones that allows movement.

Cartilage — Smooth, firm cushioning tissue on joint surfaces.

Ligament — Strong tissue connecting one bone to another.

Slide 2: Learning objectives: what you must be able to explain

Know knee extension (straightening) and flexion (bending), locking in full extension and popliteus unlocking. Identify four main ligaments, the capsule (joint sleeve), bursae (friction-reducing fluid sacs) and two menisci (cartilage pads). Read knee X-rays; recognise bipartite, high and low patellae. Link local pain and Baker’s cyst to anatomy, describe arthroscopy (camera examination), and explain the unhappy triad after injury.

Takeaway: Use anatomy to explain movement, imaging and clinical problems.

Key terms

Popliteus — Small muscle at the back of the knee that initiates unlocking.

Bipartite — Made of two separate bony parts.

Menisci — Two crescent-shaped pads between femur and tibia.

Slide 3: Lower femur: front versus back

Anterior means front; posterior means back. The medial and lateral condyles are the rounded inner and outer ends of the femur. At the back they are separated by the intercondylar notch (gap between the condyles); the front has the kneecap’s sliding surface. Above them run the medial and lateral supracondylar ridges. The adductor tubercle is the small inner bump where the hamstring part of adductor magnus attaches.

Takeaway: Intercondylar notch = posterior gap; adductor tubercle = inner attachment bump.

Key terms

Condyle — Rounded end of a bone forming a joint surface.

Medial / lateral — Toward the body’s midline / away from it.

Supracondylar — Above a condyle.

Adductor magnus — Large inner-thigh muscle; its hamstring part helps extend the hip.

Slide 4: Triceps surae: three calf components

Triceps surae means three-headed calf group: medial gastrocnemius, lateral gastrocnemius and soleus. They pull through the Achilles tendon onto the heel to point the foot down. Gastrocnemius begins above the knee, so it also bends the knee; soleus begins below it and cannot act on the knee. For the movement objective: quadriceps straightens the knee; hamstrings are the main benders, assisted by gastrocnemius, sartorius, gracilis and popliteus.

Takeaway: Gastrocnemius crosses the knee; soleus does not.

★ Movement: distinguish the calf muscles that do and do not cross the knee.
Key terms

Sural — Relating to the calf.

Tendon — Strong tissue connecting muscle to bone.

Quadriceps — Four muscles at the front of the thigh that straighten the knee.

Hamstrings — Back-of-thigh muscles that mainly bend the knee.

Memory / clinical link

Learning-objective background: sartorius is the long diagonal thigh muscle; gracilis is the thin inner-thigh muscle. Both assist knee flexion.

Slide 5: Cruciate ligaments and the two menisci

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) cross inside the knee. Their names refer to tibial attachments: the anterior cruciate ligament prevents the tibia sliding too far forwards; the posterior cruciate ligament prevents it sliding backwards. The medial meniscus is a more open C; the lateral meniscus is closer to a ring. Both deepen the tibial surfaces and spread load. The front tibial tubercle anchors the patellar ligament.

Takeaway: Anterior cruciate: stops forward tibial slide. Posterior cruciate: stops backward slide.

★ Name the ligament, then state the tibial movement it restrains.
Key terms

Cruciate — Cross-shaped; the ligaments cross one another.

Meniscus — Wedge-shaped cartilage pad spreading force across the joint.

Tibial tubercle / tuberosity — Bump on the front of the shin below the knee.

Slide 6: Tibial plateau: recognise the dissection

The tibial plateau is the broad upper surface of the shin bone, viewed here from above. Identify both menisci around the edges and both cruciate ligaments centrally. The medial collateral ligament lies on the inner side; the lateral collateral ligament lies on the outer side. Use the labelled tibial tuberosity to orient the front. The pads improve the fit between the rounded femoral condyles and relatively flat tibial surfaces.

Takeaway: Two menisci cushion; two cruciates steady the centre; two collaterals steady the sides.

Key terms

Plateau — A broad, relatively flat upper bony surface.

Collateral — Along the side of a joint.

Dissection — Anatomy exposed by carefully separating tissues.

Slide 7: Inner knee: pes anserinus and medial collateral ligament

This is the medial (inner) side of a right knee. Pes anserinus means goose’s foot: the joined tendons of sartorius, gracilis and semitendinosus spread onto the upper inner tibia. They lie superficial to (closer to the skin than) the medial collateral ligament, the broad band joining femur to tibia. That ligament resists valgus stress: force opening the inner side of the knee.

Takeaway: Pes anserinus = sartorius + gracilis + semitendinosus; ligament lies deeper.

★ Connect the three tendons to their position over the medial collateral ligament.
Key terms

Pes anserinus — Goose-foot arrangement of three tendons on the upper inner shin.

Semitendinosus — Back-of-thigh hamstring with a long tendon.

Valgus stress — A force opening the inner joint side, often after a blow to the outer knee.

Slide 8: Inner knee: meniscus and posterior support

The medial meniscus is attached to the capsule and deep fibres of the medial collateral ligament. It therefore moves less freely than the lateral meniscus and can be injured with the medial ligament. Behind the joint, the semimembranosus tendon sends an expansion upwards and outwards as the oblique popliteal ligament, strengthening the posterior capsule. Pes anserinus remains the superficial three-tendon attachment on the inner tibia.

Takeaway: Medial meniscus is tethered to the medial ligament; semimembranosus reinforces the back.

★ The medial meniscus–ligament connection helps explain combined injury.
Key terms

Semimembranosus — Broad-tendoned inner hamstring muscle.

Oblique popliteal ligament — Slanting reinforcement across the back of the knee capsule.

Capsule — Connective-tissue sleeve surrounding a joint.

Slide 9: Outer knee: four structures to separate

The lateral collateral ligament is a cord from the outer femur to the fibular head (top of the outer lower-leg bone). It resists varus stress, which opens the outer knee. The popliteus tendon passes deep to it, separating it from the lateral meniscus. Biceps femoris, the outer hamstring, also attaches to the fibular head. The iliotibial tract is a thick fascial band along the outer thigh that attaches to the outer upper tibia.

Takeaway: Lateral collateral ligament reaches fibula; iliotibial tract reaches tibia.

Key terms

Varus stress — A force opening the outer side of the knee.

Biceps femoris — Outer hamstring muscle with two heads.

Iliotibial tract / band — Thick connective-tissue strip down the outer thigh.

Fascial — Made of the connective-tissue wrapping around muscles.

Slide 10: Intercondylar eminence on a front X-ray

An anteroposterior X-ray looks from front to back. Find femur above, tibia below and fibula on the outer side. The intercondylar eminence is the raised central region between the tibial joint surfaces; its two peaks are the tibial spines. It lies between the femoral condyles and near the meniscal and cruciate attachment areas. It is a normal landmark, not a loose fragment in this image.

Takeaway: Intercondylar eminence = central raised tibial region between the two joint surfaces.

Key terms

Anteroposterior — Front-to-back imaging view.

Eminence — A raised area on a bone.

Tibial spines — Two small peaks of the intercondylar eminence.

Slide 11: A modified hinge: rotation and locking

The model shows why a low central tibial ridge allows rotation around a pivot as well as hinge-like bending. In a straight knee the ligaments tighten and the joint locks for efficient standing. During the last part of extension, a freely moving tibia rotates outwards on the femur; with the foot planted, the femur rotates inwards on the tibia. This is the screw-home mechanism. Rotation is easier again when the knee is bent.

Takeaway: Lock: tibia outwards if the foot is free; femur inwards if the foot is planted.

★ State which bone is moving when describing knee locking.
Key terms

Pivot — A point around which something turns.

Screw-home mechanism — Small rotation at the end of straightening that locks the knee.

Extension — Straightening the joint.

Memory / clinical link

Learning-objective explanation: the diagram illustrates the mechanical principle; the locking directions complete the lecture’s stated movement objective.

Slide 12: Popliteus: the key that unlocks the knee

Popliteus runs from the lateral femoral condyle and associated lateral meniscus to the back of the upper tibia. It reverses the locking rotation to start bending: with the foot free it rotates the tibia inwards; with the foot planted it rotates the femur outwards. It also draws the lateral meniscus backwards during bending, helping prevent it being trapped. Its tendon is on the outer side even though the muscle spreads across the back of the tibia.

Takeaway: Unlock: tibia inwards with a free foot; femur outwards with a planted foot.

★ Popliteus initiates unlocking by reversing the screw-home rotation.
Key terms

Popliteus — Small triangular muscle behind the knee.

Insertion — The attachment usually pulled toward the other attachment during contraction.

Flexion — Bending the knee.

Slide 13: Floor of the hollow behind the knee

The popliteal fossa is the hollow at the back of the knee. Its floor runs from the popliteal surface of the femur above, across the posterior knee capsule, to the fascia covering popliteus below. The oblique popliteal ligament reinforces the capsule and comes from semimembranosus. Learn this as three levels: bone → capsule → muscle covering. The vessels and nerves in the hollow lie superficial to this floor.

Takeaway: Floor from top to bottom: femur, capsule, fascia over popliteus.

Key terms

Popliteal — At the back of the knee.

Fossa — A hollow or depression.

Fascia — A sheet of connective tissue wrapping or separating structures.

Slide 14: Inside the capsule, outside the synovial cavity

The solid line outlines the fibrous capsule (strong outer sleeve); the dashed line outlines the synovial membrane (fluid-making inner lining). The lining folds around the cruciate ligaments, excluding them from the fluid-filled space. They are therefore intracapsular but extrasynovial: inside the sleeve, outside the synovial cavity. The side view also shows the popliteus tendon and how the lining reflects around neighbouring structures.

Takeaway: Cruciate ligaments are intracapsular but extrasynovial; synovial fluid does not bathe them.

★ Inside the joint capsule does not mean inside the synovial fluid space.
Key terms

Synovial fluid — Lubricating joint fluid made by the inner lining.

Intracapsular — Inside the fibrous joint capsule.

Extrasynovial — Outside the cavity enclosed by the synovial membrane.

Slide 15: Synovial lining, fat pad and folds

Red marks the synovial membrane around the tibial joint surfaces. The anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments sit outside its cavity. At the front, the infrapatellar fat pad cushions behind the patellar ligament; alar folds are wing-like lining folds beside it and the infrapatellar fold extends centrally. Laterally, the lining follows the popliteus tendon into a subpopliteal recess (small pocket). The lateral meniscus sits between femur and tibia.

Takeaway: Trace the red lining: it excludes cruciates and fat pad from the synovial cavity.

Key terms

Infrapatellar — Below the kneecap.

Alar — Wing-shaped.

Recess — A small extension or pocket of a cavity.

Patellar ligament — Band joining the kneecap to the tibial tuberosity; also called patellar tendon.

Slide 16: Range of movement: read the angles

The straight knee is the neutral 0° position. Flexion bends it to 90° and, in the drawing, approximately 135°. Hyperextension means moving slightly beyond straight. Actual range varies between people and with hip position; these are the diagram’s reference angles. Quadriceps produces extension; hamstrings mainly produce flexion. Rotation is possible chiefly when flexed because the fully extended knee is more tightly locked.

Takeaway: 0° = straight; 90° = right-angle bend; about 135° = deep flexion in this diagram.

Key terms

Neutral — The reference position used to measure movement.

Hyperextension — Movement beyond the straight position.

Range of movement — How far a joint can move.

Slide 17: Radiology: a three-view reading plan

Radiology means studying the body with medical imaging. The next slides use a front view to compare the inner and outer joint spaces, a side view for patellar height and soft tissues, and a skyline view for the kneecap’s joint with the femur. First orient the bones, then inspect alignment, bone edges, joint spaces and surrounding soft tissues. Cartilage itself is not directly visible on an ordinary X-ray.

Takeaway: Front = two tibiofemoral spaces; side = height and soft tissue; skyline = patellofemoral space.

Key terms

Tibiofemoral — Between tibia and femur.

Patellofemoral — Between kneecap and femur.

Joint space — X-ray gap occupied mainly by cartilage and other joint tissues.

Slide 18: Front X-ray: identify every labelled landmark

The patella overlaps the lower femur on this front view. Beneath the medial and lateral femoral condyles are the medial and lateral tibial plateaus. Centrally are the tibial spines; below are the tibia and the outer fibula. Compare both joint spaces and trace the bone outlines. The labelled overlay shows which smooth contours belong to femur versus tibia; a dark gap is normal joint space rather than empty air.

Takeaway: Use the fibula to identify the outer side, then compare both joint spaces.

Key terms

Plateau — Upper load-bearing surface of the tibia.

Contour — The outline or edge of a structure.

Patella — Kneecap, projected over the femur on a front view.

Slide 19: Side X-ray: tendons, pouch and fat pads

On the side view, the quadriceps tendon reaches the top of the patella and the patellar tendon runs from its bottom to the tibia. The suprapatellar pouch is the joint’s upward extension above the kneecap. Stars identify fat pads; their normal dark outlines help locate the pouch. Fluid collecting there can expand the pouch and separate or displace the fat pads. Also locate femur, tibia and the overlapping fibula.

Takeaway: Above patella: quadriceps tendon and suprapatellar pouch. Below: patellar tendon.

Key terms

Suprapatellar — Above the kneecap.

Pouch — An extension of the joint cavity.

Effusion — Excess fluid collected inside a joint.

Slide 20: Skyline view: the kneecap’s joint space

The skyline view looks along the patella toward its groove in the femur, usually with the knee bent. The arrows show normal spacing between the kneecap and femur on both sides. This view helps assess patellar tracking (how it sits and glides), tilt and the patellofemoral compartment. It depends on the patient being able to flex the knee; the slide explicitly makes that condition part of obtaining the view.

Takeaway: Skyline isolates the patellofemoral compartment; flexion is needed for the illustrated view.

Key terms

Skyline view — Tangential X-ray showing the kneecap facing its femoral groove.

Tracking — The path a kneecap follows while the knee moves.

Compartment — One region of a joint.

Slide 21: Patella alta and baja: opposite heights

Alta means high: the pictured kneecap sits unusually high, with a gap below it where the patellar tendon should transmit force to the tibia. A torn patellar tendon lets quadriceps pull the patella upwards and disrupts active knee extension. Baja means low; a quadriceps tendon rupture can let the intact patellar tendon pull it down. Patellar height alone is a clue, interpreted with the history and the tendon findings.

Takeaway: Patellar tendon tear → alta (high). Quadriceps tendon tear → baja (low).

★ High versus low patella helps localise which part of the extensor mechanism failed.
Key terms

Patella alta — A kneecap sitting higher than expected.

Patella baja — A kneecap sitting lower than expected.

Active extension — Straightening the knee using the person’s own muscles.

Memory / clinical link

Learning-objective background: baja is named in the objectives; the illustration itself is the alta case. Tendon-related causes are explanatory additions, not the only possible causes of abnormal height.

Slide 22: Bipartite patella: a normal two-piece variant

A bipartite patella has a small part that never fused with the main kneecap during development. The common site shown is superolateral: the upper outer corner. The slide notes mainly males and about 50% bilateral cases (both knees). Smooth, well-formed margins and the typical position favour this variant over a fresh fracture; symptoms and injury history still matter. Compare the lateral and front views before calling the separate piece abnormal.

Takeaway: A smooth upper-outer patellar fragment may be a bipartite variant rather than a fracture.

Key terms

Bipartite — In two parts.

Superolateral — Above and toward the outer side.

Bilateral — Present on both sides of the body.

Slide 23: Fabella: a small normal bone behind the knee

The fabella is a sesamoid bone (a bone developing within a tendon) in the lateral head of gastrocnemius, the outer part of the calf muscle. It appears behind the femoral condyle on the side X-ray and can also be seen on the front view. Its expected location explains why this separate small bone can be normal rather than a fracture fragment or loose body inside the joint.

Takeaway: Fabella = sesamoid in the lateral gastrocnemius head.

Key terms

Fabella — Small accessory bone behind the outer knee.

Sesamoid — A bone within a tendon.

Lateral head — Outer of a muscle’s two starting portions.

Slide 24: Clinical anatomy: use the location to narrow the structure

The clinical section applies the structures already learned. Pain at the front, inner side, outer side or back points toward different tendons, cartilage, ligaments or bursae. A bursa is a small fluid sac reducing friction; bursitis is its inflammation. The following slides connect a pain map, a posterior swelling, camera views and trauma to the relevant anatomy. Location guides an assessment but does not establish a diagnosis on its own.

Takeaway: Match the exact site of symptoms to the structure located there.

Key terms

Clinical anatomy — Using body structure to explain symptoms, examination and procedures.

Bursa — Small fluid-filled sac reducing rubbing between tissues.

Bursitis — Inflammation of a bursa.

Slide 25: Pain map: what lies at each painful spot?

Above patella: quadriceps tendinopathy (painful tendon disorder). Around/behind patella: chondromalacia patellae (softening or damage of its cartilage). Outer knee: iliotibial band syndrome from irritation near the outer femur. Inner upper tibia: pes anserinus bursitis below the joint line. Either joint line: meniscal or ligament injury. At the front tibial tubercle: Osgood–Schlatter disease, traction-related irritation at the patellar tendon attachment during growth. This map is not exhaustive.

Takeaway: Joint-line pain differs from pes anserinus pain below the inner joint line.

★ Use the marked location to distinguish tendon, bursal, cartilage and joint-line pain.
Key terms

Tendinopathy — A painful disorder of a tendon.

Chondromalacia — Softening or damage of cartilage.

Joint line — The level where femur and tibia meet.

Traction — Pulling force at an attachment.

Slide 26: Baker’s cyst: swelling behind the knee

The photograph shows a rounded swelling in the popliteal region (back of the knee). A Baker’s cyst is a fluid-filled enlargement of a posterior bursa, classically between semimembranosus and the medial head of gastrocnemius. It may communicate with the knee cavity, allowing joint fluid to fill it. The anatomy explains the position of the lump; the next slide distinguishes primary from secondary cysts.

Takeaway: Baker’s cyst = a posterior fluid-filled bursal swelling, often linked to the knee cavity.

Key terms

Cyst — A sac containing fluid or other material.

Communicate — Have a connecting passage between two spaces.

Medial gastrocnemius — Inner head of the main superficial calf muscle.

Slide 27: Baker’s cysts: primary versus secondary

The normal diagram has a small popliteal bursa behind the capsule. A primary cyst is highlighted in children and may not communicate with the joint. A secondary cyst is the more common type and communicates with the knee cavity; excess joint fluid can enter the bursa. Compare the connection, not just the size. The drawings also place the meniscus between the femur and tibia and the capsule around the joint.

Takeaway: Primary: children, may be separate. Secondary: more common, connects to the knee joint.

★ The communication with the knee cavity is the key comparison.
Key terms

Primary cyst — Here, a cyst that can arise independently of a connection to the knee cavity.

Secondary cyst — Here, a cyst connected with the knee joint and its fluid.

Popliteal bursa — A friction-reducing sac behind the knee.

Slide 28: Knee bursae: names follow their positions

Prepatellar means in front of the kneecap: inflammation here is housemaid’s knee. Superficial infrapatellar means under the skin below the kneecap, over the patellar ligament: inflammation is parson’s knee. The drawing also shows a popliteal bursa behind the joint and a bursa beneath the lateral gastrocnemius head. These sacs reduce friction where tissues move over each other. The site, rather than the traditional nickname, identifies which bursa is inflamed.

Takeaway: Housemaid’s = prepatellar. Parson’s = superficial infrapatellar.

Key terms

Prepatellar — In front of the kneecap.

Superficial infrapatellar — Close to the skin, below the kneecap.

Bursae — Plural of bursa: friction-reducing fluid sacs.

Slide 29: Arthroscopy: camera and instrument portals

Arthroscopy examines the joint with a small camera through a portal (small entry opening). The diagram labels anterolateral and anteromedial portals on either side of the patellar tendon, plus a superolateral portal above and outside the patella. One entry can carry the camera while another carries a probe or instrument. The side diagram shows both reaching the interior between femur and tibia without a large open incision.

Takeaway: Know the portals relative to the patella and patellar tendon.

Key terms

Arthroscopy — Looking inside a joint with a small camera.

Portal — A small entry opening used for the camera or an instrument.

Anteromedial / anterolateral — At the front and inner side / front and outer side.

Slide 30: Why rotate a 30° arthroscope?

The illustrated scope looks at an angle of 30° to its shaft rather than straight ahead. Rotating the shaft makes the angled view sweep around, so more of the joint can be inspected from one entry. The shaded cone and circular path show this scanning effect. The slide states that this increases the field of view by about three times; it describes this optical setup, not a threefold magnification of the tissues.

Takeaway: An angled lens plus rotation widens the area inspected through the same portal.

Key terms

Arthroscope — The camera telescope used inside a joint.

Field of view — The area visible through the instrument.

Shaft — The long main body of the instrument.

Slide 31: Arthroscopy irrigation: fluid opens the view

Two bags of lactated Ringer’s solution (sterile fluid containing salts) provide irrigation. Fluid gently distends the knee cavity (holds its walls apart) and washes away material that would obscure the camera. The slide places the bags 3–4 feet above the joint and quotes approximately 70 mmHg, a pressure measure. Height produces hydrostatic pressure; the actual pressure in the joint also depends on flow, tubing and outflow.

Takeaway: Irrigation expands the cavity and clears the view; the slide quotes about 70 mmHg.

Key terms

Irrigation — Washing through a space with fluid.

Lactated Ringer’s — A sterile solution of water and dissolved salts.

Hydrostatic pressure — Pressure produced by a column of fluid.

mmHg — Millimetres of mercury, a unit of pressure.

Slide 32: Normal versus torn meniscus through the scope

The normal view shows a smooth meniscal edge between smooth joint surfaces. The torn view shows disrupted, uneven cartilage with a fragment projecting into the space. The meniscus is the wedge-shaped load-spreading pad, not the smooth coating on the ends of the bones. A displaced tear can catch during movement and cause mechanical symptoms, such as locking or clicking; the image shows why a probe may be used to examine its stability.

Takeaway: Meniscal tear = disruption of the pad between bones; distinguish it from surface cartilage.

Key terms

Mechanical symptoms — Catching, clicking or blocked movement caused by a structure interfering with motion.

Fragment — A separated or partly separated piece.

Articular cartilage — Smooth cartilage coating the ends of bones.

Slide 33: Valgus trauma: why the inner knee opens

A blow from the outer side pushes the knee inward while the foot may stay planted: a valgus force. This opens and stretches the medial side, stressing the medial collateral ligament. Added twisting can also load the anterior cruciate ligament and medial meniscus. The sports photograph illustrates the mechanism; it does not show the internal tears directly. The anatomical links, rather than the sport itself, explain the unhappy triad on the next slide.

Takeaway: Outer-side blow → valgus stress → injury to medial stabilisers, sometimes with central injury.

★ Describe the direction of force before listing the structures at risk.
Key terms

Valgus — Here, an inward knee movement that opens the inner joint side.

Planted foot — Foot fixed against the ground during the force.

Stabiliser — A structure helping hold a joint in position.

Slide 34: Unhappy triad: the three structures

The classic unhappy triad taught here is a tear of the medial collateral ligament, medial meniscus and anterior cruciate ligament. The medial ligament and meniscus are linked, while valgus force with rotation can also rupture the anterior cruciate ligament. The slide’s “Medical meniscus” is a spelling error for medial meniscus. This is the classic teaching pattern; an actual injury can involve a different combination.

Takeaway: Classic triad: medial collateral ligament + medial meniscus + anterior cruciate ligament.

★ Learn all three members of the classic unhappy triad.
Key terms

Triad — A group of three related findings or structures.

Medial meniscus — Inner load-spreading cartilage pad.

Rupture — A complete tear of a structure.

Slide 35: Image challenge: failed quadriceps tendons

The 38-year-old man has end-stage kidney disease, difficulty straightening both knees and visible/palpable gaps above the kneecaps. The source case is bilateral quadriceps tendon rupture: both thigh muscles have lost their normal pull on the upper patella. This explains weak active extension and walking with bent knees. The kneecaps can sit low (patella baja). Kidney disease is a risk factor for weakened tendons; it does not by itself establish the diagnosis.

Takeaway: Gap above patella + failed active extension → quadriceps tendon injury; low patella supports it.

★ Connect the gap, loss of extension and low patella to the same broken force pathway.
Key terms

Bilateral — Affecting both sides.

Palpable — Able to be felt by touch.

Extensor mechanism — Quadriceps, its tendon, patella and patellar tendon transmitting the force that straightens the knee.
