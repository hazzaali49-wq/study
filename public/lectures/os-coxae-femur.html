Typed notes are reproduced as supplied; clarifications explain shorthand and correct misleading statements. Practice questions are newly written unless labelled as a lecture sample.

Slide 1: The pelvis and femur as a load-bearing unit

Body weight passes from the vertebral column through the sacrum and hip bones to the femoral heads. The acetabulum supplies a stable socket while the femoral neck positions the shaft beneath the body. Learn the bones as attachment sites and mechanical structures, not just labelled outlines.

Takeaway: Identify the view and side before naming a landmark.

Slide 2: A route through the lecture

The lecture moves from pelvic landmarks and development to the femur, hip capsule, blood supply and radiographic relationships. For each landmark ask what attaches there, what passes nearby and what changes if it is injured. The blood supply of the femoral head is a particularly important connection.

Takeaway: Structure → relationship → function → consequence.

Slide 3: The pelvic ring

Two hip bones articulate posteriorly with the sacrum and anteriorly at the pubic symphysis. Find the iliac crests, anterior superior iliac spines and anterior inferior iliac spines on the anterior view. The obturator foramina lie below the acetabula; they are distinct from the hip sockets.

Takeaway: ASIS and AIIS are separate landmarks on the anterior ilium.

Slide 4: Orienting a single hip bone

Place the acetabulum laterally, the pubis anteriorly and the greater sciatic notch posteriorly. The iliac crest is superior and the ischial tuberosity inferior-posterior. This method lets you establish the side before identifying smaller features and avoids confusing a medial view with a lateral one.

Takeaway: Socket lateral, pubis anterior, sciatic notch posterior.

Slide 5: Parts of the acetabulum

The lunate surface is the crescent-shaped articular area that contacts the femoral head. The central acetabular fossa is non-articular and contains fatty tissue, the pulvinar. The labrum deepens the rim, while the transverse acetabular ligament bridges the inferior notch. Distinguish the load-bearing surface from the central hollow.

Takeaway: Lunate surface articulates; acetabular fossa does not.

Slide 6: Three bones become one hip bone

The ilium forms the broad superior portion, the ischium the posteroinferior portion, and the pubis the anteroinferior portion. All contribute to the acetabulum. In the developing skeleton they meet at triradiate cartilage before fusion produces the adult os coxa.

Takeaway: One adult hip bone develops from ilium, ischium and pubis.

Slide 7: Primary ossification centres

Each of the three components has a primary ossification centre. Bone expands from these centres through the cartilaginous model during development. The lecture gives a broad prenatal timing; the main idea is that the adult fused bone has three developmental components rather than a single starting centre.

Takeaway: Primary centres form the main parts of the bone.

Slide 8: Secondary ossification centres

The red markings indicate later-developing centres around the iliac crest, anterior inferior iliac spine, ischial tuberosity, pubic symphyseal region and acetabular region. Some are apophyses at major traction sites. Before fusion, a growth region can look separate on imaging; age and position help distinguish development from injury.

Takeaway: A normal unfused growth centre should not automatically be called a fracture.

Slide 9: A child's pelvic radiograph

This case is followed by slides identifying the ischiopubic synchondrosis. Compare the two sides of the inferior pelvic ring and find the junction between ischium and pubis. A growing child's pelvis contains cartilage and fusion sites absent from an adult image, so adult expectations can mislead interpretation.

Takeaway: Read paediatric imaging with developmental anatomy in mind.

Slide 10: Locating the ischiopubic junction

The arrow points to the junction of the inferior pubic ramus and ischial ramus. It is separate from the triradiate cartilage in the acetabulum. Trace the boundary of the obturator foramen to orient yourself: this junction lies along its inferior border.

Takeaway: Ischiopubic junction and triradiate cartilage are different growth regions.

Slide 11: Ischiopubic synchondrosis

A synchondrosis is a cartilaginous junction. The ischiopubic synchondrosis unites developing pubic and ischial components and can appear asymmetrically enlarged during maturation. The slide links this region to the preceding pain case; the image alone should not be used to label every asymmetry as pathological.

Takeaway: Recognise the expected developmental junction before interpreting asymmetry.

Slide 12: The ischial tuberosity as a hamstring origin

The ischial tuberosity is the 'sitting bone' and the origin of semimembranosus, semitendinosus and the long head of biceps femoris. Their distal course behind the hip and knee explains hip extension and knee flexion. The short head of biceps starts on the femur and is an important exception.

Takeaway: Most true hamstrings begin at the ischial tuberosity.

Slide 13: Typical pelvic sexual dimorphism

The figures contrast common adult patterns. A typically female pelvis has a wider subpubic angle and roomier inlet and outlet; a typically male pelvis is often narrower and deeper. These are population tendencies with substantial overlap, rather than rules that identify every individual.

Takeaway: Learn comparative morphology without treating the categories as absolute.

Slide 14: Pelvic inlet shapes

The historical Caldwell-Moloy scheme describes gynecoid, android, anthropoid and platypelloid inlet shapes. Think respectively of a rounded form, a more heart-shaped form, a longer anteroposterior oval and a transversely wide flattened oval. It is a descriptive framework; actual birth mechanics depend on more than the inlet outline.

Takeaway: Describe the outline and dominant diameter rather than memorising a name alone.

Slide 15: An asymmetrical Naegele pelvis

The image illustrates oblique pelvic asymmetry associated with underdevelopment of one sacral ala. Compare the two sides and see how unequal posterior support alters the inlet. This example shows why the pelvic ring must be considered as a linked structure: a change in one region affects overall geometry.

Takeaway: Naegele pelvis is an example of oblique pelvic asymmetry.

Slide 16: Sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments

The sacrospinous ligament runs to the ischial spine; the sacrotuberous ligament runs to the ischial tuberosity. Together with the bony sciatic notches, they create the greater and lesser sciatic foramina. Their similar names become easier if you anchor each to its named ischial attachment.

Takeaway: Spine → sacrospinous; tuberosity → sacrotuberous.

Slide 17: From bony notches to neurovascular passages

The gluteal image places nerves and vessels through the openings defined by bone and ligament. Use piriformis as a landmark in the greater sciatic foramen. The sciatic nerve normally exits inferior to it, while the superior gluteal bundle passes superiorly. Revisit this map in the gluteal lecture.

Takeaway: Pelvic osteology explains the routes of gluteal nerves and vessels.

Slide 18: Pelvic inlet and outlet

The inlet is the superior entrance to the true pelvis, bounded by the pelvic brim. The outlet is the inferior opening related to the pubic arch, ischial tuberosities, sacrotuberous ligaments and coccyx. In side view, notice that the two planes are not simply parallel horizontal circles.

Takeaway: Do not confuse the pelvic inlet with the inferior outlet.

Slide 19: Why fetal descent involves rotation

The dimensions and orientation of the birth canal change along its curved path. The diagrams illustrate how descent and rotation align the fetal head with these changing spaces. Use them to connect inlet, midpelvis and outlet anatomy; the exact process cannot be reduced to one static pelvic measurement.

Takeaway: A three-dimensional curved canal requires changing orientation during descent.

Slide 20: Anterior femur and muscle compartments

Identify the head medially, greater trochanter laterally and patellar surface distally on the anterior femur. The small cross-section uses A, M and P for anterior, medial and posterior compartments. Bony orientation determines how muscle groups are arranged around the shaft.

Takeaway: Anterior distal femur has the patellar surface.

Slide 21: Posterior femur and the linea aspera

The linea aspera is the prominent longitudinal ridge on the posterior shaft and an important muscle-attachment region. Distally, the medial and lateral condyles are separated by the intercondylar fossa. Compare this with the smooth anterior patellar surface to distinguish views quickly.

Takeaway: Linea aspera and intercondylar fossa identify the posterior view.

Slide 22: Head, neck and trochanters

The head articulates with the acetabulum and contains the fovea for the ligament of the head. The neck connects it to the shaft. Greater and lesser trochanters are attachment landmarks; the intertrochanteric line is anterior and the intertrochanteric crest posterior. These distinctions also help locate capsular attachments.

Takeaway: Line anterior, crest posterior; head medial, greater trochanter lateral.

Slide 23: The fibrous hip capsule

The capsule is reinforced by iliofemoral, pubofemoral and ischiofemoral ligaments. Their spiral arrangement becomes tighter in extension and more relaxed in flexion. This provides passive stability during standing while allowing a wider range of movement when the hip flexes.

Takeaway: Hip ligaments are generally taut in extension.

Slide 24: The iliofemoral ligament

This strong, Y-shaped anterior reinforcement runs from the region of the anterior inferior iliac spine and acetabular rim toward the intertrochanteric line. It particularly limits excessive extension, helping maintain upright posture without constant maximal muscle activity.

Takeaway: Iliofemoral ligament: strong anterior check to hyperextension.

Notes in your uploaded PDF
  • supports anteriorly to stop anterior dislocation

Note explained: The iliofemoral ligament reinforces the anterior capsule and checks excessive extension; it contributes to stability rather than making anterior dislocation impossible.

Slide 25: Ligament of the femoral head

Also called ligamentum teres, it attaches to the fovea on the femoral head and to the acetabular notch/transverse ligament region. It carries a small arterial contribution, especially relevant in childhood, but is not the main adult blood supply. Do not confuse it with the external capsular ligaments.

Takeaway: The artery in ligamentum teres is not the dominant adult supply.

Slide 26: How blood reaches the femoral head

Retinacular vessels ascend along the femoral neck beneath the capsule. The medial circumflex femoral artery is the main source for the adult head, with other contributions. Because these vessels hug the neck, a displaced intracapsular fracture can interrupt them even though the head remains within the socket.

Takeaway: Medial circumflex → retinacular vessels → femoral head.

Notes in your uploaded PDF
  • the most important source of blood flow to the femur

Note explained: More precisely, medial circumflex-derived retinacular vessels are the dominant adult femoral-head supply, not the only blood supply to the entire femur.

Slide 27: Fracture location predicts vascular risk

A subcapital fracture lies just below the femoral head and is intracapsular. A pertrochanteric fracture crosses the trochanteric region. The key contrast is the risk to retinacular vessels with a displaced neck fracture, which can impair head perfusion and lead to osteonecrosis. Do not assume all proximal femoral fractures carry identical risk.

Takeaway: Relate the fracture line to the capsule and retinacular vessels.

Slide 28: Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease

The lecture's young-child case illustrates osteonecrosis of the developing femoral head. Loss of blood supply can lead to collapse and later remodelling. Compare this with the adolescent growth-plate slip later in the lecture: the age group and anatomical process differ even though both can cause limp and hip pain.

Takeaway: Perthes: childhood femoral-head osteonecrosis.

Notes in your uploaded PDF
  • spontaneous death of head of femur

Note explained: Perthes disease is childhood osteonecrosis of the femoral head; 'spontaneous death' is shorthand that omits the vascular process.

Slide 29: The distal femur

The condyles articulate with the tibia; anteriorly the patellar surface articulates with the patella. Epicondyles lie outside the articular surfaces and provide attachment landmarks. The adductor tubercle lies superior to the medial epicondyle and receives the hamstring part of adductor magnus.

Takeaway: Medial adductor tubercle is an attachment landmark, not an articular surface.

Slide 30: Neck-shaft angle: coxa vara and coxa valga

The angle of inclination is measured between the femoral neck and shaft in the coronal plane. About 125 degrees is the lecture's adult reference, with age and individual variation. Coxa vara means a reduced angle; coxa valga means an increased angle. The slide's knock-knee and bowleg labels are oversimplified: coxa describes the hip, whereas genu valgum/varum describes the knee.

Takeaway: Do not confuse neck-shaft angle with knee alignment or femoral torsion.

Note explained: Use reduced versus increased femoral neck-shaft angle to define coxa vara/valga. Hip and knee alignment should not be treated as interchangeable terms.

Slide 31: Acetabular orientation on an AP radiograph

The acetabulum faces laterally and somewhat anteriorly. In a standard AP image the anterior and posterior wall outlines project differently; the slide uses this to explain why the posterior wall is seen more laterally. Pelvic positioning affects these lines, so first check image orientation before interpreting them.

Takeaway: An AP image is a projection of a three-dimensional socket.

Slide 32: Femoral torsion or anteversion

Look down the femoral shaft and compare the neck axis with the line through the distal condyles. The neck normally points somewhat anterior to that distal reference, called femoral anteversion. This is an axial-plane relationship and is different from the coronal neck-shaft angle.

Takeaway: Inclination is a coronal angle; torsion is an axial angle.

Slide 33: Femoral ossification

The shaft forms from a primary centre, while the head, trochanters and distal epiphysis develop secondary centres. The distal femoral centre typically appears around birth and is a traditional marker of skeletal maturity. Appearance and fusion ages vary, so a single centre is not an exact biological clock.

Takeaway: Distinguish a shaft centre, an epiphysis and a traction apophysis.

Slide 34: The adolescent hip and the slipped epiphysis

The 'ice cream slipping off the cone' image illustrates slipped capital femoral epiphysis. The slip occurs through the growth plate between the head and neck in a skeletally immature adolescent. Compare the head-neck alignment on both sides and contrast the growth-plate process with Perthes disease.

Takeaway: SCFE affects the proximal femoral physis, not the shaft.

Slide 35: SCFE follow-up

The follow-up image shows the fixation used in the lecture case. Its anatomical purpose is to stabilise the head-neck relationship across the growth plate. For study, focus on recognising where the slip occurred and why this threatens normal joint mechanics; the particular management shown is a case example.

Takeaway: Compare the growth-plate alignment before and after fixation.

Slide 36: What the Q angle measures

The Q angle is formed by the quadriceps line of pull and the line of the patellar tendon. The lecture gives example values of 14 degrees and 17 degrees for male and female groups; these are not universal cutoffs. It is a frontal-plane alignment measure around the patella, not a femoral neck angle.

Takeaway: Q angle concerns the extensor mechanism at the knee.

Slide 37: Constructing the Q angle

Draw one line from the anterior superior iliac spine to the centre of the patella and another from the patellar centre to the tibial tuberosity. The angle between them estimates the lateral component of the extensor pull. Measurements vary with posture and technique, so keep the landmark method consistent.

Takeaway: ASIS → patella → tibial tuberosity.

Slide 38: Why a larger Q angle can favour lateral patellar displacement

A greater lateral component of quadriceps pull can contribute to lateral patellar tracking or instability. The trochlear groove and soft-tissue restraints also matter, so the angle alone does not determine whether a patella dislocates. Use the radiographs to distinguish the patella's position from tibiofemoral alignment.

Takeaway: Patellar tracking depends on several bony and soft-tissue factors.

Slide 39: Nélaton's line and Bryant's triangle

These surface-landmark constructions compare the position of the greater trochanter relative to the pelvis. Nélaton's line joins ASIS and ischial tuberosity; Bryant's triangle uses a perpendicular from ASIS and a horizontal relation to the greater trochanter in the supine position. An elevated trochanter changes the expected relationships and can suggest proximal shortening.

Takeaway: Compare both sides and distinguish relative trochanteric position from total limb length.

Slide 40: Lecture sample question: posterior dislocation

The intended answer is C, ischiofemoral ligament, which reinforces the posterior capsule. The iliofemoral ligament is primarily anterior and the pubofemoral reinforcement is anteroinferior. Lacunar and sacrotuberous ligaments are not capsular reinforcements of the hip. Real dislocations can injure multiple structures; this question tests the main posterior reinforcement.

Takeaway: Posterior hip capsule → ischiofemoral ligament.

Practice questions

Which acetabular region is articular?

Lunate surface. The crescent-shaped lunate surface articulates with the femoral head. The central fossa contains non-articular tissue.

Which ligament attaches to the ischial spine?

Sacrospinous. The sacrospinous ligament reaches the spine; the sacrotuberous ligament reaches the tuberosity.

Which artery provides the dominant adult femoral-head supply through retinacular vessels?

Medial circumflex femoral. The medial circumflex femoral contribution is dominant. Its retinacular vessels are vulnerable near the femoral neck.

Coxa vara means which change?

Decreased femoral neck-shaft angle. Coxa vara describes a reduced neck-shaft angle. Q angle, torsion and knee alignment are separate measurements.

In the lecture's adolescent case, SCFE occurs through which structure?

Proximal femoral growth plate. SCFE is a slip through the proximal femoral physis, affecting head-neck alignment.

Lecture sample: which capsular reinforcement is associated with the posterior hip in a posterior dislocation question?

Ischiofemoral. C, ischiofemoral. The question tests the posterior capsular reinforcement; real injuries may damage multiple structures.
