Slide 1: The gluteal region and hip

This region combines large muscles that move and stabilise the hip with deep routes for nerves and vessels leaving the pelvis. Build a layered map: skin and surface landmarks, gluteal muscles, short rotators, then the hip capsule and neurovascular structures.

Takeaway: Learn the layers before memorising the lists.

Slide 2: The lecture's connected questions

The topics can be organised around four questions: what moves the hip, what keeps the pelvis level, what passes through the sciatic foramina, and what supplies the femoral head? Use these questions to connect the muscle diagrams, nerve routes and clinical examples.

Takeaway: Muscles, passages and blood supply form one regional map.

Slide 3: Surface anatomy of the buttock

The intergluteal cleft separates the buttocks; the gluteal sulcus marks the lower skin fold. The fold is a surface feature and does not precisely trace the entire lower edge of gluteus maximus. The iliac crests provide a superior landmark and help estimate a lumbar level.

Takeaway: Skin folds are useful landmarks but are not exact muscle boundaries.

A line joining the highest points of the iliac crests approximates the L4 level or L4-L5 interval, with individual variation. The slide links this surface landmark to the lower lumbar region, below the usual adult termination of the spinal cord. Its purpose here is anatomical orientation, not a standalone procedure guide.

Takeaway: Iliac crests give an approximate, not infallible, lumbar landmark.

Slide 5: Three gluteal muscles, two main roles

Gluteus maximus is the large superficial muscle and a powerful hip extensor. Medius lies deeper and more lateral; minimus lies deep to medius. Medius and minimus are major abductors and pelvic stabilisers. Relating depth to function makes the names more useful than size alone.

Takeaway: Maximus powers extension; medius and minimus stabilise single-leg stance.

Slide 6: Gluteal lines on the ilium

The posterior, anterior and inferior gluteal lines mark regions of muscle origin. Gluteus medius arises between the posterior and anterior lines; minimus between the anterior and inferior lines; maximus includes the area behind the posterior line. Use these boundaries to reconstruct the layered muscle arrangement.

Takeaway: Medius between posterior/anterior lines; minimus between anterior/inferior lines.

Slide 7: Gluteus medius exposed

The broad fan-shaped muscle arises from the outer ilium and converges toward the greater trochanter. With its femoral attachment fixed during standing, contraction can pull on the pelvis instead of moving the thigh. This is the mechanical basis for its stabilising role.

Takeaway: A muscle can move either attachment depending on which end is fixed.

Slide 8: Posterior dissection and muscle depth

One side shows superficial coverage while the other exposes deeper structures. Follow maximus first, then medius and the deep rotators. Gluteus maximus extends and laterally rotates the hip and receives the inferior gluteal nerve; medius and minimus receive the superior gluteal nerve.

Takeaway: Inferior gluteal nerve → maximus; superior gluteal nerve → medius/minimus/TFL.

Slide 9: Gluteus maximus and the iliotibial tract

Gluteus maximus transmits force through two insertions: superficial fibres into the iliotibial tract and deeper fibres into the gluteal tuberosity of the femur. The slide's wording about 'lower fibres' is incomplete; superficial/deep is the more useful distinction. TFL also tensions the tract, which continues to the lateral proximal tibia.

Takeaway: Remember both insertions, rather than assigning every lower fibre to the tract.

Slide 10: Why gluteal injection landmarks matter

The diagram places the sciatic nerve away from the traditional superolateral gluteal region. The anatomical lesson is that a poorly chosen site can injure a major nerve lying deep to gluteus maximus. Clinical site selection depends on the prescribed technique and local guidance; the figure is a regional relationship map.

Takeaway: The sciatic nerve is a key structure to avoid in the deep gluteal region.

Slide 11: Gluteus medius: attachments and action

Medius arises from the external ilium between the posterior and anterior gluteal lines and inserts on the lateral surface of the greater trochanter. Superior gluteal nerve supply enables abduction and stance-phase pelvic control. Its anterior fibres assist medial rotation; the action varies with fibre direction and hip position.

Takeaway: Origin on ilium, insertion on greater trochanter, superior gluteal nerve.

Slide 12: Gluteus minimus: the deepest gluteal abductor

Minimus arises between the anterior and inferior gluteal lines and attaches to the anterior aspect of the greater trochanter. It works with medius to abduct and stabilise the hip and contributes to medial rotation. Both muscles are supplied by the superior gluteal nerve.

Takeaway: Minimus lies deep to medius and shares its nerve supply.

Slide 13: Unilateral abductor weakness

When the affected hip bears weight, its abductors may fail to hold the opposite side of the pelvis up. The pelvis drops away from the weak stance side; compensatory trunk lean may occur toward it. The diagram also illustrates altered femoral and lumbar positioning, which can accompany impaired control.

Takeaway: Name the supporting hip first, then describe the contralateral pelvic drop.

Slide 14: The pelvic inlet in a lateral view

The dotted line follows the pelvic brim, separating greater from lesser pelvis. In this view, also locate the obturator foramen inferiorly and the greater and lesser sciatic notches posteriorly. These are different boundaries and openings, even though they appear close together in a two-dimensional illustration.

Takeaway: Pelvic brim, obturator foramen and sciatic foramina are distinct landmarks.

Slide 15: The iliopectineal line

The radiographic iliopectineal line follows the anterior column of the acetabulum and relates to the pelvic brim. Compare the anatomical outline with the X-ray projection. A continuous anatomical border creates a smooth projected line; interruption may therefore flag a bony injury for further assessment.

Takeaway: Follow a radiographic line as a contour, not as a separate physical cord.

Slide 17: Deep pelvic relationships

With levator ani removed in this teaching diagram, the pelvic-wall structures are easier to see. Identify obturator internus on the lateral wall and piriformis posteriorly, then relate the sacral plexus to piriformis. The drawing simplifies three dimensions, so use bone and muscle landmarks before following nerves.

Takeaway: Piriformis provides a posterior landmark for the sacral plexus.

Slide 18: Internal iliac arterial divisions

The internal iliac artery supplies pelvic and gluteal territories through branches commonly grouped into anterior and posterior divisions. The superior gluteal artery usually arises from the posterior division; the inferior gluteal and internal pudendal usually arise from the anterior division. Branch patterns vary, so learn the territories as well as a schematic tree.

Takeaway: Superior gluteal: usually posterior division; inferior gluteal/internal pudendal: usually anterior.

Slide 19: Pelvic vessels and their exit routes

Gluteal and internal pudendal branches instead leave through the greater sciatic foramen, following the regional pathways shown in the next slides.

Slide 20: Greater and lesser sciatic foramina

Sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments convert the sciatic notches into foramina. Piriformis passes through the greater foramen and divides its available passage into superior and inferior parts. The lesser foramen transmits the obturator internus tendon and routes structures toward the perineum.

Takeaway: Piriformis divides the greater foramen; obturator internus turns through the lesser.

Slide 21: Piriformis as the central landmark

On the posterior view, locate piriformis before the nerves. The superior gluteal bundle passes above it, while the sciatic nerve and several other bundles pass below. Beneath piriformis are the gemelli, obturator internus tendon and quadratus femoris. This creates an ordered map from superior to inferior.

Takeaway: Find piriformis first; then sort structures above and below.

Slide 22: The short external rotators

Piriformis, obturator internus, superior and inferior gemelli, quadratus femoris and obturator externus contribute to lateral rotation and hip stability. The posterior view especially shows the first five around the greater trochanter and intertrochanteric region; obturator externus lies more anteriorly. Their short paths help hold the femoral head in the socket.

Takeaway: These muscles stabilise the joint as well as rotate the femur.

Slide 23: The anatomy behind the obturator sign

A pelvic appendix can lie near obturator internus and irritate tissues adjacent to it. Stretching the muscle may then provoke pain. The sign is an anatomical association, not a definitive diagnosis by itself; it depends on the appendix's position and the cause of inflammation.

Takeaway: Pelvic inflammation can hurt when an adjacent muscle is stretched.

Slide 24: Why internal rotation stretches obturator internus

Obturator internus is a lateral rotator of the hip. Internally rotating the flexed thigh moves opposite to that action and can stretch it. Trace its tendon from the internal pelvic surface, around the lesser sciatic notch and toward the greater trochanter to understand the change in direction.

Takeaway: Opposing a muscle's action helps explain a stretch test.

Slide 25: What passes above and below piriformis

Above piriformis are the superior gluteal nerve and vessels. Below are the inferior gluteal bundle, sciatic nerve, posterior femoral cutaneous nerve, nerves to quadratus femoris and obturator internus, and the pudendal nerve with internal pudendal vessels. The pudendal bundle then hooks around the ischial spine region and enters the perineum through the lesser foramen.

Takeaway: Superior gluteal bundle is the key above-piriformis group.

Slide 26: Piriformis and sciatic nerve variants

The usual sciatic nerve passes below piriformis, but its tibial and common fibular components can have variant relationships to the muscle. Takeaway: Know the usual route and recognise that variants exist.

Slide 33: Trochanteric and cruciate anastomoses

These arterial connections link branches around the proximal femur. The trochanteric network includes circumflex femoral and gluteal contributions near the greater trochanter and neck. The cruciate network links inferior gluteal, transverse circumflex and first perforating branches more inferiorly. Do not confuse a regional collateral network with a guarantee that the femoral head will remain perfused after injury.

Takeaway: Name the network, its region and its contributing arteries.

Slide 34: Why femoral-head blood supply matters

The adult femoral head relies predominantly on retinacular vessels derived from the medial circumflex femoral artery. Their close relationship to the neck makes them vulnerable in displaced intracapsular fractures and dislocation. Loss of perfusion can produce osteonecrosis even when other nearby arterial connections remain present.

Takeaway: Neck injury → retinacular vessel damage → risk of femoral-head osteonecrosis.

Practice questions

Which nerve supplies gluteus maximus?

Inferior gluteal. Inferior gluteal supplies maximus. Superior gluteal supplies medius, minimus and TFL.

Where does gluteus medius insert?

Greater trochanter. Medius inserts on the lateral aspect of the greater trochanter, enabling hip abduction and pelvic stabilisation.

Which tendon turns through the lesser sciatic foramen?

Obturator internus. Obturator internus turns around the lesser sciatic notch and exits through the lesser foramen toward the femur.

Which bundle passes above piriformis?

Superior gluteal bundle. The superior gluteal nerve and vessels pass above piriformis. The major remaining structures listed on the slide pass below.

The pudendal bundle turns near which bony landmark before entering the lesser sciatic foramen?

Ischial spine. It hooks around the ischial spine/sacrospinous ligament region after leaving through the greater foramen.

Why can a displaced intracapsular femoral neck fracture cause head osteonecrosis?

It can disrupt retinacular vessels. The retinacular vessels run along the neck; disrupting them can deprive the head of its main supply.
