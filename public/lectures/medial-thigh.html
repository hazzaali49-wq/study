Medial Compartment of the Thigh

Slide 1: Medial thigh: the adductor compartment

The medial compartment mainly draws the thigh toward the midline. It also links pelvic structures to the femur and, through gracilis, to the medial tibia. Learn its layered muscles together with the obturator nerve, regional vessels and adductor canal.

Takeaway: Adduction is the shared action, with important attachment and nerve exceptions.

Slide 2: The route through this lecture

The lecture connects muscle anatomy to avulsion and fracture displacement, then follows obturator and femoral neurovascular pathways. Pes anserinus links three compartments, while corona mortis and obturator hernia show why deep pelvic relationships matter. The added note translates corona mortis as 'crown of death'.

Takeaway: Use the clinical examples to explain the underlying relationships.

Slide 3: Where the medial compartment sits

In this thigh cross-section the medial compartment lies toward the body's midline, between the anterior quadriceps and posterior hamstring groups. Deep fascia and septa organise these spaces. Its main nerve is obturator, compared with femoral anteriorly and sciatic posteriorly.

Takeaway: Identify the side and view before deciding which region is medial.

Slide 4: The adductor family and its exceptions

The slide groups adductor longus, pectineus and gracilis anteriorly, brevis in the middle and magnus posteriorly. Most receive obturator supply. Pectineus is mainly supplied by the femoral nerve, sometimes with an additional obturator contribution; the hamstring part of magnus receives tibial-division sciatic supply.

Takeaway: The slide's shared-innervation rule is useful, but not universal.

Slide 5: Muscle layers explain nerve divisions

Adductor brevis is a useful central landmark. The anterior division of the obturator nerve generally runs in front of it, and the posterior division behind it. The layered drawing therefore predicts the nerve routes and helps distinguish a superficial adductor from the deeper magnus.

Takeaway: Adductor brevis separates the main obturator nerve divisions.

Slide 6: Obturator nerve: route and distribution

The obturator nerve arises from L2-L4, follows the pelvic wall and enters the thigh through the obturator canal at the superior part of the obturator foramen. Its anterior division supplies longus, gracilis and usually brevis; the posterior supplies obturator externus and the adductor part of magnus. Supply to brevis can vary. It also gives articular and variable cutaneous branches.

Takeaway: Canal, not the whole membrane-covered foramen, is the neurovascular passage.

Slide 7: Why hip pathology may be felt at the knee

The hip and knee share segmental and peripheral articular innervation, including obturator contributions from L2-L4. Sensory input can converge centrally, so the perceived site of pain may differ from its source. A classic teaching example is hip pathology presenting as knee pain; the slide's reversed wording should not obscure that principle.

Takeaway: Shared sensory pathways allow referred pain between joint regions.

Slide 8: Obturator internus versus externus

Both muscles are lateral rotators but lie on opposite sides of the obturator membrane. Internus receives the nerve to obturator internus from the sacral plexus; externus receives the posterior division of the obturator nerve. The obturator sign concerns stretching internus near pelvic inflammation, not an appendix literally trapped inside the muscle.

Takeaway: Similar names do not imply the same nerve supply.

Note explained: Obturator internus can be stretched near an inflamed pelvic appendix; the appendix is not literally 'stuck in' the muscle. Internus and externus have different nerve supplies.

Slide 9: Pectineus and adductor longus

Pectineus runs from the superior pubic ramus/pecten toward the pectineal line of the femur and helps flex and adduct the hip. Adductor longus runs from the pubic body to the middle region of the linea aspera and chiefly adducts. The annotation draws attention to traction at a concentrated bony attachment, which can produce an avulsion in suitable circumstances.

Takeaway: Pectineus is mainly femoral-nerve supplied; longus is obturator supplied.

Note explained: A bony avulsion occurs when traction pulls off an attachment fragment. Concentrated force and skeletal maturity affect the injury pattern.

Slide 10: Adductor magnus and gracilis

Magnus is broad and reaches extensive femoral attachments, with a distinct hamstring part ending at the adductor tubercle. Gracilis is the long superficial medial strap running from the pubis to the medial proximal tibia. Because gracilis crosses the knee, it can assist knee flexion as well as hip adduction.

Takeaway: Gracilis reaches the tibia; magnus ends on the femur.

Slide 11: Adductor brevis and obturator externus

Brevis runs from the pubic body/inferior ramus to the proximal linea aspera and adjacent femoral region. Externus arises around the external obturator membrane and passes behind the femoral neck to the trochanteric fossa. Their different lines of pull explain adduction by brevis and lateral rotation/stabilisation by externus.

Takeaway: Obturator externus inserts in the trochanteric fossa.

Slide 12: Map the attachments rather than memorising isolated names

Follow the coloured origins on the pubis and ischium to the elongated femoral insertion regions. Gracilis continues beyond the femur to the tibia. The annotation about an attachment 'ripping off' refers to avulsion: a tendon or ligament pulls a fragment from its bony attachment, rather than simply tearing across the bone shaft.

Takeaway: Avulsion occurs at a traction attachment.

Slide 13: Pes anserinus: three compartments meet

Sartorius, gracilis and semitendinosus insert together on the anteromedial proximal tibia, forming the goose-foot pattern. Their nerves are respectively femoral, obturator and the tibial division of sciatic. An underlying bursa reduces friction; inflammation can cause local medial knee pain below the joint line.

Takeaway: Sartorius-Gracilis-Semitendinosus: three muscles, three nerve pathways.

Slide 14: Intertrochanteric fracture and muscular pull

A fracture between the trochanters separates bone segments carrying powerful muscle attachments. Muscle forces can alter fragment alignment, which is why understanding attachments matters when interpreting the image and fixation shown. This extracapsular region differs from the intracapsular femoral neck and its retinacular blood supply.

Takeaway: Describe fracture location and fragment forces before discussing the implant.

Note explained: Muscle forces influence alignment, but fixation decisions depend on the complete fracture and clinical context, not muscle strength alone.

Slide 15: Subtrochanteric fracture: predicting displacement

A break below the lesser trochanter leaves the proximal fragment influenced by iliopsoas, abductors and external rotators; adductors pull on the shaft fragment. This can create flexion, abduction, external rotation and shortening patterns. The annotation that plates can never be used is too absolute: fixation depends on fracture and patient factors.

Takeaway: Muscle attachments explain deforming forces; no single implant rule covers every fracture.

Notes in your uploaded PDF
  • cannot use plate because it will bend due to very strong muscles

Note explained: The statement that a plate can never be used is incorrect. Intramedullary fixation is common, but plate constructs have selected uses; the teaching point is the strong deforming forces.

Slide 16: Cephalomedullary fixation

The radiograph shows a nail within the femoral canal with fixation extending toward the head-neck region. It illustrates a common strategy for controlling proximal-fragment alignment and transmitting load along the femur. Study where the device lies in relation to the canal, neck and fracture rather than treating the image as a universal treatment instruction.

Takeaway: Cephalomedullary describes fixation linking the proximal head-neck region to the medullary canal.

Slide 17: Obturator nerve in the medial thigh

Trace the nerve from the obturator canal into its anterior and posterior branches around adductor brevis. The anterior division is more superficial, between longus and brevis; the posterior lies deeper, between brevis and magnus after supplying externus. The diagram also shows articular and cutaneous distribution beyond the main muscle branches.

Takeaway: Use brevis as the divider when locating the branches.

Slide 18: Internal iliac artery and obturator supply

The obturator artery usually arises from the anterior division of the internal iliac artery and travels toward the obturator canal. Its route connects pelvic circulation to the medial thigh. Compare it with superior gluteal supply from the posterior division and with external iliac flow toward the femoral artery.

Takeaway: Obturator is usually an internal-iliac branch, but variants matter.

Slide 19: Pelvic arterial relationships

Follow the common iliac into internal and external iliac systems before identifying smaller branches. The internal system supplies pelvic and gluteal territories; the external continues beneath the inguinal ligament into the femoral artery. These neighbouring systems can communicate over the superior pubic ramus.

Takeaway: A connection between arterial territories can cross a surgical landmark.

Slide 20: Corona mortis

Corona mortis describes an arterial or venous connection between obturator and external iliac/inferior epigastric systems crossing the superior pubic ramus. The slide gives a prevalence estimate for its example; reported frequency varies with definition and whether veins are included. Its importance is the position where injury can cause substantial bleeding.

Takeaway: Remember the superior pubic ramus and the two connected vascular systems.

Notes in your uploaded PDF
  • communication between the obturator and external iliac

Note explained: The connection may involve an inferior epigastric branch of the external iliac system and may be arterial or venous. Its position over the superior pubic ramus is the key hazard.

Slide 21: Why the vascular variant matters near a femoral hernia

The diagram places the anomalous vessel near the femoral ring and superior pubic ramus. A vessel in this location can be vulnerable during operations in the region, including femoral hernia repair. 'Crown of death' is a warning about a potentially hazardous vascular relationship, not a prediction of inevitable death.

Takeaway: Know the relationship to the femoral ring, not just the dramatic name.

Slide 22: Profunda femoris and the femoral shaft

The deep femoral artery supplies major thigh branches, including perforators close to the femur. A shaft fracture or a projecting orthopaedic screw can injure these nearby vessels. Trace the artery relative to bone to understand why a problem arising in the skeleton can cause vascular injury.

Takeaway: Close bone-vessel relationships explain bleeding risk.

Slide 23: The adductor canal or Hunter's canal

This passage runs from the femoral triangle apex toward the adductor hiatus. Vastus medialis forms the anterolateral boundary, adductor longus/magnus the posterior boundary, and a fascial roof lies beneath sartorius. Femoral vessels and the saphenous nerve travel through the region. The vessels pass through the hiatus to become popliteal; the saphenous nerve exits separately and does not pass through that hiatus.

Takeaway: Canal and hiatus are different; saphenous nerve leaves before the vessels turn posteriorly.

Slide 24: Blood supply of the medial compartment

Obturator branches reach the compartment through the obturator canal, while femoral/profunda branches contribute from the thigh. The adductor magnus is also closely related to perforating branches passing through it toward the posterior compartment. Relate each vessel to its origin and route instead of assigning the whole compartment one exclusive artery.

Takeaway: Medial-thigh supply has obturator and femoral-system contributions.

Slide 25: Lecture sample question: the insertion exception

The correct answer is C, gracilis. Adductor magnus, longus, brevis and pectineus insert on the femur; gracilis extends to the proximal medial tibia as part of pes anserinus. This also explains why gracilis can act at the knee while the other listed muscles do not cross it.

Takeaway: Gracilis is the tibial-insertion exception in this list.

Slide 26: Incarcerated obturator hernia

The CT case illustrates bowel herniating through the obturator canal. The narrow neck can trap bowel and produce obstruction; proximity to the obturator nerve can also produce medial-thigh pain. Identify the canal region and the herniated bowel in the labelled case instead of relying on the rarity note alone.

Takeaway: A small anatomical passage can trap bowel and affect an adjacent nerve.

Slide 27: Where an obturator hernia emerges

The diagram places the hernia between obturator externus and pectineus after it passes through the obturator canal. Pectineus covers the region anteriorly, helping explain why this is a deep hernia rather than an obvious superficial groin lump. Relate the canal to both muscles to remember the route.

Takeaway: Obturator canal → deep to pectineus, adjacent to obturator externus.

Practice questions

Which muscle is mainly supplied by the femoral nerve despite its medial-compartment association?

Pectineus. Pectineus is mainly femoral-nerve supplied; additional obturator supply can occur.

Which muscle usually separates the anterior and posterior divisions of the obturator nerve?

Adductor brevis. The anterior division runs in front of brevis and the posterior division behind it.

Which set forms pes anserinus?

Sartorius, gracilis, semitendinosus. Sartorius, gracilis and semitendinosus converge on the anteromedial proximal tibia.

Where is the important corona mortis connection located?

Over the superior pubic ramus. It connects obturator and external iliac/inferior epigastric systems near the superior pubic ramus.

Which structure leaves the adductor canal without passing through the adductor hiatus?

Saphenous nerve. The saphenous nerve exits separately; the femoral vessels pass through the hiatus and continue as popliteal vessels.

Lecture sample: all the listed adductor-compartment muscles insert into the femur except which?

Gracilis. C, gracilis. It inserts on the medial proximal tibia as part of pes anserinus.
