Slide 1: Posterior thigh: movement and a nerve corridor

The posterior thigh contains the hamstring group and the major sciatic pathway toward the leg. Study it as both a motor compartment and a route connecting the gluteal region to the popliteal fossa. Proximal attachments explain hip action; distal attachments explain knee action and the fossa's upper boundaries.

Takeaway: Follow muscles and nerves from buttock to knee.

Slide 2: The lecture's learning sequence

First identify the hamstrings and their attachments. Then connect action to innervation and blood supply, use dissections and ultrasound to recognise the structures, and finish with the popliteal fossa. A useful answer explains how position produces function rather than listing names alone.

Takeaway: Attachments → actions → supply → relationships.

Slide 3: Revisiting the thigh compartments

The cross-section shows quadriceps anteriorly, adductors medially and hamstrings posteriorly. Their main nerve associations are femoral, obturator and sciatic respectively. The posterior group is supplied mainly by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve, with the short head of biceps femoris supplied by its common fibular division.

Takeaway: The compartment rule has a specific biceps short-head exception.

Slide 4: Who belongs to the posterior group?

Semitendinosus, semimembranosus and the long head of biceps femoris are the classic two-joint hamstrings. The short head of biceps lies in the region but does not cross the hip. The ischial part of adductor magnus has hamstring-like origin and innervation but inserts on the femur, so it does not flex the knee.

Takeaway: Do not assume every posterior-thigh muscle crosses both joints.

Slide 5: Biceps femoris: two heads, different origins

The long head begins at the ischial tuberosity, whereas the short head begins on the posterior femur. They join toward an insertion mainly on the fibular head. Both flex the knee and laterally rotate the flexed leg; only the long head extends the hip. Their nerve supply differs: tibial division for long head, common fibular division for short head.

Takeaway: Short head has no hip action because it does not cross the hip.

Slide 6: Intramuscular tendons of biceps femoris

The illustration shows that tendons extend into the muscle belly rather than ending at a neat muscle-tendon boundary. This arrangement transfers force along a broad interface. It helps explain why injuries may involve the musculotendinous region or an internal tendon, not just the external tendon at a bony attachment.

Takeaway: Muscle and tendon overlap structurally within the muscle belly.

Slide 7: The two medial hamstrings

Semitendinosus has a long, cord-like distal tendon reaching the medial proximal tibia as part of pes anserinus. Semimembranosus has a broad proximal tendon and inserts on the posterior medial tibial condyle. Both extend the hip, flex the knee and medially rotate the flexed leg; both receive tibial-division sciatic supply.

Takeaway: Tendinosus: long distal tendon; membranosus: broad proximal tendon.

Slide 8: Semimembranosus and the oblique popliteal ligament

An expansion from the semimembranosus tendon passes superolaterally across the posterior knee capsule to contribute to the oblique popliteal ligament. This supports the posterior capsule. It is distinct from the popliteus muscle lying more inferiorly in the floor of the fossa.

Takeaway: OPL is a capsular reinforcement related to semimembranosus, not another hamstring.

Slide 9: The hamstring part of adductor magnus

The ischial or hamstring part originates from the ischial tuberosity and inserts at the adductor tubercle on the femur. It extends the hip and is supplied by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve. The adductor part has a different attachment pattern and mainly obturator-nerve supply.

Takeaway: Hamstring part crosses the hip but ends above the knee.

Slide 10: Tracing proximal and distal attachments

This overview connects the ischial origin region to the medial and lateral distal hamstrings. Trace each muscle continuously rather than identifying isolated labels: biceps travels laterally to the fibular head, while semitendinosus and semimembranosus travel medially to the tibia. That divergence creates the upper sides of the popliteal fossa.

Takeaway: Lateral tendon → biceps; medial tendons → the two semis.

Slide 11: The ischial tuberosity footprint

Semimembranosus occupies a separate origin area from the conjoint tendon of semitendinosus and the long head of biceps femoris. On the upper tuberosity, the semimembranosus footprint is more superolateral and the conjoint footprint more inferomedial. The hamstring part of adductor magnus arises more inferiorly.

Takeaway: Three nearby attachment areas should not be collapsed into one undifferentiated origin.

Slide 12: Conjoint tendon on dissection

The dissection relates the conjoint hamstring origin to the ischial tuberosity, sciatic notches and adductor magnus. 'Conjoint' means shared: semitendinosus and the long head of biceps femoris have a common proximal tendon. Semimembranosus has a separate proximal tendon beside it.

Takeaway: Shared proximal tendon does not mean shared distal insertion.

Slide 13: The raphe of semitendinosus

The raphe is a fibrous seam within semitendinosus. Follow the muscle from the conjoint origin into its belly and long distal tendon, and distinguish it from the adjacent biceps long and short heads. Internal connective-tissue landmarks help identify muscles in dissection and ultrasound.

Takeaway: A raphe is an internal fibrous seam, not an extra muscle.

Slide 14: Posterior dissection: locating the sciatic nerve

The reflected gluteus maximus exposes the proximal hamstrings and the sciatic nerve lateral to their ischial origin region. More distally the nerve descends deep to the long head of biceps femoris on the adductor magnus region. The numbered key lets you relate each muscle to the nerve rather than memorising them separately.

Takeaway: Sciatic nerve descends deep to biceps long head.

Slide 15: Ultrasound: the Mercedes-Benz landmark

The transverse ultrasound uses the arrangement of biceps femoris, semitendinosus and deeper structures to create a recognisable pattern. First check probe orientation and depth, then use the labelled BF, ST, AM and tendon landmarks. A pattern name is an aid to orientation, not a substitute for identifying the actual tissues.

Takeaway: Orient the probe, identify depth, then name the labelled structures.

Slide 16: Recognising the semitendinosus raphe on ultrasound

The raphe provides a connective-tissue landmark within semitendinosus. Compare the scan level marked on the thigh with the cross-sectional image, then locate the neighbouring biceps femoris and deeper adductor magnus. Echogenic appearance depends on the imaging plane, so anatomy should guide recognition.

Takeaway: An internal landmark helps confirm which muscle occupies a scan region.

Slide 17: Surface form and underlying hamstrings

The posterior-thigh photograph is paired with the underlying muscle map. The medial and lateral hamstrings become more distinct as their tendons approach the knee. Surface contours are influenced by fat and muscle bulk, so use them as orientation aids and confirm the deeper relationships on the diagram.

Takeaway: Surface shape and deep anatomical borders are related but not identical.

Slide 18: Why a sciatic block affects distal territories

The sciatic nerve contains tibial and common fibular components that supply much of the leg and foot. Blocking this pathway can therefore interrupt sensation and movement in its distal territories. The medial leg/foot supplied by the saphenous nerve belongs to the femoral pathway and is a key exception. This slide illustrates distribution, not a technique to reproduce.

Takeaway: Sciatic territory is extensive, but it does not include all lower-limb skin.

Slide 19: Hamstring actions follow their joint crossings

Muscles passing behind the hip extend it; muscles passing behind the knee flex it. Semitendinosus and semimembranosus medially rotate the flexed leg, while biceps femoris rotates it laterally. The slide calls hip extension 'weak', but contribution varies with posture and task; the hamstrings are important hip extensors.

Takeaway: Joint crossing predicts action; short-head biceps cannot extend the hip.

Slide 20: The torn-hamstring example

The source includes a video reference and a still that does not itself reveal the full injury. The anatomical link is rapid force production and lengthening across the hip and knee, which can load the hamstring muscle-tendon unit heavily. The exact damaged structure cannot be diagnosed from this slide alone.

Takeaway: Use the example to connect two-joint anatomy with injury mechanisms.

Slide 21: Perforating arteries from profunda femoris

Branches of the profunda femoris pass through adductor magnus to supply the posterior compartment. The anterior and posterior views show the same route from different sides. The name 'perforating' describes their passage through the muscle to reach the compartment behind it.

Takeaway: Profunda femoris → perforating branches → posterior thigh.

Slide 22: Posterior-thigh skin supply

The posterior femoral cutaneous nerve supplies much of the skin over the posterior thigh, with neighbouring cutaneous contributions shown around its borders. Do not give this skin territory directly to the sciatic nerve merely because the sciatic travels underneath it. The two nerves are close but have different functions.

Takeaway: Posterior thigh skin: posterior femoral cutaneous nerve.

Slide 23: Surface course of the sciatic nerve

The surface line uses pelvic and proximal-femoral landmarks to approximate the nerve's descent into the posterior thigh. Relate the line to the ischial tuberosity and greater trochanter, then follow it toward the upper popliteal region. It is an anatomical projection; actual depth and division level vary.

Takeaway: A surface marking approximates a deep nerve's route.

Slide 24: The sciatic bed

After leaving below piriformis, the sciatic nerve crosses posterior to the obturator internus/gemelli region and quadratus femoris before descending on adductor magnus. Gluteus maximus and then the biceps long head overlie parts of its route. Naming bed and covering structures distinguishes what is deep from what is superficial.

Takeaway: Sciatic bed lies deep to the nerve; its muscular covering lies superficial.

Slide 25: Boundaries of the popliteal fossa

The diamond-shaped fossa is bounded superolaterally by biceps femoris and superomedially by semimembranosus with semitendinosus. The gastrocnemius heads form the lower boundaries. Within the central bundle, the usual superficial-to-deep order is tibial nerve, popliteal vein, popliteal artery; the common fibular nerve follows the lateral boundary.

Takeaway: Upper borders are hamstrings; lower borders are gastrocnemius.

Slide 26: Roof and superficial structures

Skin, superficial fascia and popliteal fascia form the roof. The small saphenous vein and cutaneous nerves are associated with this superficial region before deeper connections. The tibial nerve, vein and artery are contents beneath the roof; do not list them as layers of the roof itself.

Takeaway: Separate roof layers from neurovascular contents.

Slide 27: Floor of the popliteal fossa

From superior to inferior, the floor includes the popliteal surface of the femur, the posterior knee capsule with its reinforcing structures, and fascia over popliteus. The artery lies deepest against this floor, with vein and tibial nerve more superficial. A posterior approach therefore encounters different layers in sequence.

Takeaway: Floor: femur → posterior capsule → popliteus region.

Slide 28: Reasoning through a popliteal swelling

Use the tissue of origin to organise the differential: skin/soft tissue, vein, artery, lymph node, joint, bursa or bone. A popliteal cyst commonly relates to the gastrocnemius-semimembranosus bursa; an aneurysm arises from an artery. The slide is a differential list, not evidence that every posterior-knee lump is a cyst.

Takeaway: Name the possible tissue of origin before naming a diagnosis.

Slide 29: Popliteal artery aneurysm

The lecture highlights the popliteal artery as a common location for peripheral arterial aneurysm. Expansion can affect adjacent structures, while thrombosis or embolisation can threaten distal perfusion. Its percentage figures have an unspecified denominator and should not be interpreted as a share of all aortic and peripheral aneurysms combined.

Takeaway: Know the anatomical location and consequences rather than memorising unqualified percentages.

Practice questions

Which posterior-thigh muscle head does not extend the hip?

Biceps femoris short head. The short head originates from the femur and does not cross the hip.

Which division supplies biceps femoris short head?

Common fibular division of sciatic. This is the important nerve-supply exception among the posterior-thigh muscles.

Which two muscles share a conjoint proximal tendon?

Semitendinosus and biceps long head. Semitendinosus and the long head of biceps femoris share the conjoint tendon at the ischial tuberosity; semimembranosus has a separate footprint.

Which nerve primarily supplies skin over the posterior thigh?

Posterior femoral cutaneous. The posterior femoral cutaneous nerve supplies this skin territory and is distinct from the nearby sciatic nerve.

What is the usual central superficial-to-deep order in the popliteal fossa?

Tibial nerve, vein, artery. The tibial nerve is most superficial of this central bundle, then the vein, then the artery against the floor.

Which muscle lies in the inferior part of the popliteal fossa floor?

Popliteus. The floor progresses from femur to posterior capsule and then the popliteus region.
