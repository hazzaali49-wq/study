Osteology of the Leg & Foot

Slide 1: Leg and foot: start with the bones

Osteology means studying bones. In anatomical language, the leg is the region between knee and ankle. The tibia is the large inner shin bone and carries most load; the fibula is the slender outer bone and provides muscle and ligament attachments. Both contribute to the ankle socket. Use the original specimen to follow the broad upper end, shaft and expanded lower end of the tibia.

Takeaway: Tibia = main inner shin bone; fibula = slender outer bone.

Key terms

Osteology — Study of bones.

Medial / lateral — Toward the body midline / away from it.

Slide 2: Learning objectives: a route through the lecture

Identify tibial and fibular landmarks, nutrient openings and growth centres. Revise knee surfaces, cruciate ligaments and patella. Distinguish the inferior tibiofibular syndesmosis from the ankle hinge; identify seven tarsal bones and ankle ligaments. Apply these relationships to bone access, Osgood-Schlatter disease, infection, Pott’s contributions and Weber ankle-fracture patterns.

Takeaway: Connect each landmark to growth, movement, blood supply or injury.

Key terms

Foramen — An opening in bone.

Ossification — Formation of bone.

Syndesmosis — A fibrous connection between bones with limited movement.

Slide 3: Right leg: anterior landmarks

The tibia lies medially and the fibula laterally. On the tibia locate the medial and lateral condyles (upper rounded joint ends), intercondylar eminence (central rise), tibial tuberosity (front bump for the patellar ligament), sharp anterior border and flat medial subcutaneous surface. The interosseous membrane links the shafts. At the ankle, the tibia forms the medial malleolus and the fibula the lateral malleolus. The fibular head is at the knee end.

Takeaway: Flat inner tibia is close to skin; the two malleoli are the ankle bumps.

Key terms

Subcutaneous — Immediately beneath the skin.

Malleolus — Bony ankle projection.

Interosseous membrane — Strong sheet spanning between the bones.

Slide 4: Right leg: posterior landmarks

The back view reverses the apparent positions of the bones. Identify the fibular styloid process, head and neck; on the tibia find the oblique soleal line and posterior surface. Soleus, a calf muscle, attaches along this line. Inferiorly the medial and lateral malleoli surround the talus, with the calcaneus below. The talus transmits load into the foot; the calcaneus is the heel bone.

Takeaway: Use the soleal line to recognise the posterior tibia.

Key terms

Soleal line — Back-of-tibia ridge associated with soleus attachment.

Styloid process — Pointed bony projection.

Talus / calcaneus — Ankle bone beneath the shin / heel bone.

Slide 5: Nutrient artery: preserve the bone’s blood supply

A nutrient artery enters the tibial shaft through a nutrient foramen, usually on its posterior surface near the soleal-line region. It supplies the marrow and inner shaft. The close-up shows the opening and canal rather than a fracture. The slide stresses preserving this vessel during surgery because injury can compromise bone blood supply and healing.

Takeaway: A small posterior opening carries an important artery.

Trace artery → opening → inner bone; connect preservation to healing.
Key terms

Nutrient artery — Artery supplying the inside of a bone.

Medullary cavity — Central marrow space within the shaft.

Slide 6: Direction of nutrient canals

The mnemonic is “From the knee I flee, to the elbow I grow.” Nutrient canals in the tibia and femur generally point away from the knee, while those of the humerus, radius and ulna generally point toward the elbow. The radius specimen illustrates the upper-limb comparison; the tibia specimen shows a canal directed down its shaft. NF marks the nutrient foramen.

Takeaway: For this lecture, tibial nutrient canal points away from the knee.

Key terms

Nutrient canal — Oblique tunnel carrying a vessel into bone.

Radius — Forearm bone on the thumb side.

Slide 7: How a long bone grows

The shaft develops from a primary ossification centre; secondary centres form in the bone ends. A growth plate separates each developing end from the shaft and allows lengthening. The blue areas represent cartilage not yet replaced by bone. The tibial drawing marks the upper centre around birth, the lower centre around the end of the first year, the tuberosity region around 12 years, and union around 16–18 years.

Takeaway: Bone lengthens at cartilage growth plates before they fuse.

Key terms

Diaphysis — Shaft of a long bone.

Epiphysis — End region of a growing long bone.

Growth plate — Cartilage layer where lengthening occurs.

Added context / clarification

The ages are the schematic lecture values, not fixed deadlines for every individual.

Slide 8: Law of ossification: first appears, last unites

The lecture’s rule concerns secondary centres in the epiphyses: the centre that appears first usually unites with the shaft last. This gives that end a longer growth period. Do not confuse appearance of a centre with fusion: a visible bony centre can remain separated from the shaft by a growth plate for years. The next examples compare the tibia with the fibula.

Takeaway: First secondary centre to appear → normally last to fuse.

Keep appearance and union as two separate events.
Key terms

Ossification centre — Site where bone formation begins.

Union / fusion — Joining of the bony end to the shaft.

Slide 9: Apply the rule to the tibia

This repeated growth sequence lets you apply the rule visually. The upper tibial centre is shown at birth, the lower one by about one year, and the tuberosity centre later. Follow the blue growth regions until they disappear as the ends join the shaft. The earlier upper centre is associated with the major growing end near the knee; the drawing gives approximate late-adolescent union ages.

Takeaway: Read the sequence: cartilage → centre appears → growth plate persists → fusion.

Key terms

Secondary centre — Bone-forming centre in an end region.

Cartilage — Firm flexible supporting tissue that can be replaced by bone.

Slide 10: Fibular head: the lecture’s exception

The fibula has secondary centres at its upper head and lower ankle end. In this diagram the lower centre appears around the first year and unites around 15–17 years; the upper centre appears around 3–4 years but unites later, around 17–19 years. Thus the head appears later and also fuses later, contrary to the usual first-appears/last-unites rule.

Takeaway: Fibular head: later appearance AND later union in the lecture diagram.

Know why the fibular head is the named exception.
Key terms

Fibular head — Expanded upper end of the outer lower-leg bone.

Distal / proximal — Farther from the trunk / nearer the trunk.

Added context / clarification

This is the lecture’s teaching mnemonic; actual developmental timing varies.

Slide 11: How the knee bones fit

The side view places the femur above the tibia and the patella in front. The rounded femoral end meets the relatively flat tibial surface. The tubercle of the tibia is the front bump below the joint, not the kneecap itself. The patella slides against the front of the femur; the next slide adds the cartilage, lining and tendon chain.

Takeaway: Patella is in front; tibial tubercle is below the joint.

Key terms

Patella — Kneecap.

Tibial tubercle — Front shin-bone bump receiving the patellar ligament.

Slide 12: Capsule, cartilage and the straightening chain

Quadriceps tendon reaches the upper patella; the patellar ligament continues to the tibial tubercle. The capsule is the joint’s outer sleeve and the synovial membrane its inner fluid-producing lining. Hyaline articular cartilage covers the contacting bone ends. Above the patella, the suprapatellar bursa/pouch is an upward extension of the joint cavity beneath the quadriceps tendon.

Takeaway: Quadriceps → tendon → patella → patellar ligament → tibia.

Key terms

Hyaline articular cartilage — Smooth low-friction covering on contacting bone surfaces.

Synovial membrane — Inner lining producing lubricating joint fluid.

Suprapatellar — Above the kneecap.

Slide 13: Front and back of the knee capsule

The front drawing shows the quadriceps tendon, patella, patellar ligament and tibial tubercle in one line of pull. The capsule attaches around the joint margins while the front is reinforced by the extensor apparatus. Behind the joint, the oblique posterior ligament is the oblique popliteal ligament, reinforcing the posterior capsule. It is an expansion associated with semimembranosus, a hamstring tendon.

Takeaway: Recognise front tendon chain versus back capsular reinforcement.

Key terms

Capsule — Strong sleeve enclosing the joint.

Oblique popliteal ligament — Diagonal reinforcement across the back of the knee.

Slide 14: Top of the right tibia

Viewed from above, the tibia has medial and lateral articular surfaces separated by a central non-articular region. Articular means involved in contact at a joint; non-articular means not a smooth load-bearing contact surface. The tibial tubercle identifies the anterior edge. Cruciate and meniscal attachments occupy parts of the central intercondylar area rather than covering the whole plateau.

Takeaway: Tubercle = front; two surfaces = inner and outer plateaus.

Key terms

Tibial plateau — Broad upper surface of the shin bone.

Intercondylar — Between the rounded joint-end regions.

Slide 15: Cruciate ligaments restrain tibial sliding

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) restrains the tibia sliding forwards relative to the femur. Posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) restrains it sliding backwards. The two side drawings isolate these opposite movements; anterior is the front and posterior the back. These descriptions use movement of the tibia while the femur is the reference, avoiding confusion when the thigh moves over a planted foot.

Takeaway: Anterior cruciate: forward tibial slide; posterior cruciate: backward tibial slide.

Name the moving bone and direction before naming the ligament.
Key terms

Cruciate — Crossing; the two ligaments cross.

Restrain — Limit excessive movement.

Slide 16: LAMP: remember the femoral attachments

LAMP means Lateral–Anterior, Medial–Posterior. The anterior cruciate ligament attaches to the medial-facing surface of the lateral femoral condyle; the posterior cruciate ligament attaches to the lateral-facing surface of the medial femoral condyle. The drawing looks toward the condyles and shows the crossing bands. These femoral attachment landmarks do not change the fact that the ligament names refer to their tibial attachment positions.

Takeaway: Lateral femoral condyle → anterior cruciate; medial femoral condyle → posterior cruciate.

Do not reverse the femoral condyles in LAMP.
Key terms

Condyle — Rounded joint end of a bone.

LAMP — Lateral–Anterior; Medial–Posterior femoral attachment mnemonic.

Slide 17: Menisci and cruciates from above

The medial meniscus is a broad open C; the lateral meniscus forms a more nearly closed ring. These cartilage pads sit around the tibial surfaces and their ends attach centrally. Between them, locate the anterior and posterior cruciate attachments. The labelled tibial tubercle marks the front so you can orient the image before deciding which attachment is anterior.

Takeaway: Medial meniscus = open C; lateral = more circular.

Key terms

Meniscus — Firm wedge-shaped cartilage pad spreading joint load.

Anterior / posterior — Front / back.

Slide 18: Why the menisci are wedge-shaped

The upper drawing places the medial and lateral menisci between femur and tibia. Below, the cross-section shows a thick outer edge and thin inner edge: the wedge deepens the relatively flat tibial surface to fit the rounded femoral condyle. This increases contact area, spreads load and improves stability rather than merely filling an empty gap.

Takeaway: Menisci improve fit between round femur and flatter tibia.

Key terms

Cross-section — A cut-across view showing internal shape.

Contact area — Amount of surface sharing a load.

Slide 19: Open knee: identify the supporting structures

The patella is reflected downward to expose the joint. Identify anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments centrally, medial and lateral menisci on the tibia, and collateral ligaments at the sides. The medial collateral ligament is related to the medial meniscus. Laterally, popliteus tendon intervenes near the lateral meniscus and lateral collateral ligament; the biceps tendon reaches the fibular head. The patellar ligament remains below the reflected patella.

Takeaway: Centre = cruciates; pads = menisci; sides = collaterals.

Key terms

Reflected — Folded back to expose deeper structures.

Semilunar cartilage — Older name for a meniscus: a crescent-shaped cartilage pad.

Slide 20: Upper tibia: connect both menisci

This labelled top view repeats the menisci, anterior and posterior cruciate attachments, and anterior tibial tuberosity. The transverse ligament of the knee connects the anterior regions of the two menisci. Identify the tuberosity first, then follow the medial open C and lateral near-ring. The small central ligament attachments belong to the non-articular region between the two tibial plateaus.

Takeaway: Transverse ligament connects the fronts of the menisci.

Key terms

Transverse — Running across from side to side.

Tuberosity — Rough bony prominence for a strong attachment.

Slide 21: Right patella: front versus back

The rough anterior surface receives the quadriceps attachment and continuity of the extensor tissues. The pointed apex faces down and gives attachment to the patellar ligament. On the posterior surface, identify separate facets contacting the medial and lateral femoral condyles; the lateral facet is generally larger. Orient the patella using its apex and smooth posterior articular surface before deciding the side.

Takeaway: Patellar apex points down; smooth facets face the femur.

Key terms

Facet — Small joint-contact surface.

Apex — Pointed end.

Ligamentum patellae — Latin name for the patellar ligament.

Slide 22: Bipartite patella: three locations

A bipartite patella develops when a separate bone-forming centre fails to unite with the main kneecap. The slide’s classification shows type I at the inferior pole (5%), type II at the lateral margin (20%) and type III at the superolateral pole (75%), the commonest. Superolateral means upper outer. The circled fragment is therefore a developmental variant, rather than automatically evidence of an acute fracture.

Takeaway: Type III, superolateral, is the commonest bipartite pattern.

Distinguish a developmental fragment from a fracture using the full context.
Key terms

Bipartite — Formed of two separate bony parts.

Inferior pole — Lower tip of the kneecap.

Slide 23: Transverse patellar fracture: restore the chain

A transverse fracture runs across the patella. Quadriceps pull can separate the upper fragment from the lower fragment attached to the patellar ligament, interrupting knee extension. The illustration and radiograph show a figure-of-eight tension-band construct with longitudinal fixation. Its anatomical purpose is to hold the pieces together and restore the extensor chain. The particular fixation method depends on the fracture.

Takeaway: Fixation restores continuity of the knee-straightening mechanism.

Key terms

Transverse fracture — Break running across a bone.

Extensor mechanism — Muscle-tendon-kneecap chain that straightens the knee.

Slide 24: Collateral ligaments: tibia versus fibula

The medial ligament runs from the medial femoral epicondyle to the medial tibia; the lateral ligament runs from the lateral femoral epicondyle to the fibular head. An epicondyle is an attachment prominence above a condyle. The side views also label patella and tibial tubercle. Remember the different lower attachments: medial collateral is tibial; lateral collateral is fibular.

Takeaway: Medial collateral → tibia; lateral collateral → fibula.

Key terms

Epicondyle — Attachment bump above a joint condyle.

Collateral ligament — Supporting band along a joint’s side.

Slide 25: Ankle: a hinge in a bony socket

The lower tibia and fibula form a socket around the talus. The tibial medial malleolus and fibular lateral malleolus form its side walls. The ankle hinge mainly permits dorsiflexion (foot toward shin) and plantarflexion (pointing the foot down). Turning the sole inward and outward occurs mainly at joints beneath and in front of the talus, rather than being the ankle hinge’s principal motion.

Takeaway: Tibia + fibula grip talus; hinge moves foot up and down.

Key terms

Mortise — Socket formed by the lower tibia and fibula.

Dorsiflexion / plantarflexion — Foot toward shin / foot pointing downward.

Slide 26: Inferior tibiofibular syndesmosis

A syndesmosis is a fibrous joint: strong ligaments and the interosseous ligament bind the lower tibia and fibula with limited movement. This maintains the ankle socket while allowing slight give. The interosseous membrane continues toward this distal stabilising region. The slide’s articular-cartilage clause refers to a small lower contact zone/recess; it does not reclassify the main fibrous syndesmosis as an ordinary freely moving synovial joint.

Takeaway: Syndesmosis = fibrous support with small movement, preserving the ankle socket.

Separate the tibia-fibula stabilising joint from the talus-bearing ankle hinge.
Key terms

Syndesmosis — Bones linked mainly by strong fibrous tissue.

Interosseous ligament — Strong ligament between the adjacent bones.

Added context / clarification

Anatomical clarification: a limited cartilage-lined contact region may exist at the base of the syndesmosis; this is compatible with its fibrous classification. See the linked cadaveric study.

Slide 27: Seven tarsal bones

Tarsals are the seven bones of the rear and middle foot. The talus lies beneath the leg; the calcaneus is the heel below it. The navicular lies in front of the talus on the medial side; the cuboid lies in front of the calcaneus laterally. Three cuneiforms (medial, intermediate and lateral) lie in front of the navicular and behind the first three metatarsals, the long forefoot bones.

Takeaway: Talus, calcaneus, navicular, cuboid, three cuneiforms = seven.

Key terms

Tarsal — One of the seven rear/midfoot bones.

Cuneiform — Wedge-shaped; three bones in front of the navicular.

Metatarsals — Five long bones between the tarsals and toes.

Slide 28: Talus: load-bearing surfaces

The talus sits under the tibia, between the two malleoli. Its upper dome contacts the tibia; side facets contact medial and lateral malleoli. The head points forward toward the navicular. Beneath it are calcaneal contact surfaces. The matching calcaneal drawings label the surfaces for the talus and the anterior surface for the cuboid, showing how ankle load is transmitted onward into the foot.

Takeaway: Upper talus meets tibia; head meets navicular; lower surfaces meet calcaneus.

Key terms

Talar dome — Rounded upper weight-bearing surface of the talus.

Articular surface — Cartilage-covered region where bones contact.

Slide 29: Spring ligament supports the talar head

The talar head lies above a supporting band between the sustentaculum tali and navicular. The sustentaculum tali is a medial shelf of the calcaneus; navicular is the inner midfoot bone in front of the talus. The spring ligament spans between them and helps support the talar head and medial longitudinal arch. The diagram links the bony shelf to a soft-tissue support rather than leaving the head unsupported.

Takeaway: Spring ligament: calcaneal shelf → navicular, supporting the talar head.

Key terms

Sustentaculum tali — Medial heel-bone shelf supporting the talus.

Spring ligament — Plantar calcaneonavicular ligament beneath the talar head.

Slide 30: Lateral ankle: anterior talofibular and calcaneofibular

The lateral view and dissection label tibia, fibula, talus and calcaneus. Anterior talofibular ligament joins the lateral malleolus to the anterior talus; calcaneofibular ligament descends from the fibula to the calcaneus. The small schematic also identifies posterior talofibular and anterior tibiofibular ligaments: distinguish a tibia-fibula syndesmotic band from the fibula-to-foot lateral stabilisers.

Takeaway: Anterior talofibular → talus; calcaneofibular → heel bone.

Trace each ligament to its two named bones.
Key terms

Talofibular — Connecting talus and fibula.

Calcaneofibular — Connecting calcaneus and fibula.

Added context / clarification

Added clinical link: anterior talofibular ligament is commonly injured in an inversion sprain, when the sole turns inward.

Slide 31: Posterior ankle ligaments

From behind, the posterior talofibular ligament runs from the fibular malleolar region toward the posterior talus, while calcaneofibular continues toward the calcaneus. Compare this view with the previous anterior talofibular label: the two talofibular bands lie on different sides of the talus. These three named fibula-to-foot bands form the main lateral ankle ligament group.

Takeaway: Lateral group: anterior talofibular, calcaneofibular, posterior talofibular.

Key terms

Posterior talofibular ligament — Band between posterior talus and fibula.

Posterior view — Looking at the ankle from behind.

Slide 32: Medial ankle: deltoid ligament

The broad fan-shaped medial or deltoid ligament starts at the tibial medial malleolus. Its fibres reach the navicular, calcaneal/spring-ligament region and talus. Identify tibionavicular, tibiocalcaneal, anterior tibiotalar and posterior tibiotalar components by their named destinations. The coloured specimen separates the bundles. Together they support the medial ankle and resist excessive eversion, turning the sole outward.

Takeaway: Deltoid is the strong medial fan from tibia to several foot supports.

Key terms

Deltoid ligament — Broad medial ankle ligament complex.

Eversion — Turning the sole outward.

Tibiotalar — Connecting tibia and talus.

Slide 33: Clinical anatomy: apply the landmarks

This section applies the preceding bones and ligament relationships to emergency access, growth-related pain, bone infection, historical eponyms and ankle-fracture classification. For each case, locate the named structure first and then explain how its blood supply, growth plate or mechanical attachment produces the finding.

Takeaway: Use location → structure → mechanism.

Key terms

Eponym — Name of a condition taken from a person.

Clinical anatomy — Using anatomical relationships to explain patient findings.

Slide 34: Intraosseous infusion: why the medial tibia is accessible

Intraosseous means into bone. The illustration places emergency access through the flat anteromedial proximal tibial surface, near the tuberosity, with a 2 cm reference below it. This region is close beneath the skin, so access can reach the vascular marrow cavity when rapid circulatory access is needed. Identify medial condyle, tuberosity and the flat surface as orientation landmarks.

Takeaway: Flat anteromedial tibia provides access to vascular marrow.

Key terms

Intraosseous — Within bone; here access to the marrow cavity.

Infusion — Delivery of fluid or medication.

Added context / clarification

The image teaches anatomical landmarks, not a complete insertion procedure; actual site selection depends on age, device and clinical protocols.

Slide 35: Osgood-Schlatter disease: traction at a growing attachment

The patellar ligament pulls on the tibial tuberosity whenever quadriceps contracts. In a growing adolescent, repeated traction can irritate this developing attachment and produce pain, swelling and fragmentation. The arrow points to the tuberosity on the side X-ray. The slide emphasises adolescent boys; the condition can also occur in girls. This is a growth-related attachment problem rather than a torn cruciate ligament.

Takeaway: Adolescent pain at tibial tuberosity → repeated patellar-ligament traction.

Key terms

Traction — A pulling force.

Apophysis — Developing bony prominence where a tendon or ligament attaches.

Slide 36: Osteomyelitis: living bone, dead fragment and new shell

Osteomyelitis is infection of bone. The upper tibial metaphysis is a common childhood site; the lecture names Staphylococcus aureus as a frequent organism. Follow healthy bone (A), periosteum (B), involucrum (C), sinus tract (D) and sequestrum (E). A sequestrum is dead separated bone with lost blood supply; involucrum is new bone formed around the infected focus. A sinus tract is a drainage passage to the surface.

Takeaway: Sequestrum = dead bone; involucrum = surrounding new bone.

Differentiate dead central fragment from the new surrounding bone.
Key terms

Metaphysis — Widened shaft-end region next to a growth plate.

Periosteum — Living tissue covering the outside of bone.

Nidus — Persistent focus that can maintain infection.

Added context / clarification

The heading’s “most commonly” is an oversimplification: acute blood-borne infection often affects long-bone metaphyses, including the proximal tibia and distal femur.

Slide 37: Percival Pott: four contributions

Pott’s fracture refers here to a bimalleolar ankle fracture (both ankle bumps). Pott’s disease is tuberculosis affecting the spine. Pott’s puffy tumour is frontal-bone osteomyelitis with an overlying swelling, not an ordinary neoplasm. His occupational-cancer observation linked chimney-sweep soot exposure with scrotal carcinoma. Match each illustration to ankle, spine, forehead and occupational exposure respectively.

Takeaway: Pott: ankle fracture, spinal tuberculosis, frontal-bone infection, occupational scrotal cancer.

Key terms

Bimalleolar — Involving both inner and outer ankle malleoli.

Tuberculosis — Infection caused by tuberculosis bacteria.

Carcinoma — A cancer arising from lining or surface cells.

Slide 38: Weber ankle fractures: level relative to syndesmosis

Classify the fibular fracture relative to the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis: Weber A is below, B at its level, and C above. The normal diagram shows an intact socket; the other drawings compare fibular fracture level and medial injury. Higher patterns raise concern for syndesmotic disruption, but stability also depends on medial and posterior structures. A type B fracture is not automatically stable or automatically unstable.

Takeaway: Weber A below, B at, C above the syndesmosis.

Locate the fibular break, then assess associated medial/syndesmotic injury.
Key terms

Weber classification — Grouping distal fibular fractures by their level relative to the syndesmosis.

Ankle stability — Ability to maintain normal joint alignment under load.

Added context / clarification

Source clarification: Weber is a location classification; overall stability is not determined by the letter alone.
