Slide 1: Lumbar plexus: a lower-back nerve network

Lumbar means lower back; a plexus is a network where nerve fibres mix and divide into named nerves. The lumbar plexus forms mainly from the front branches of spinal nerves L1–L4, within the back part of psoas major, a deep hip-flexing muscle beside the spine. Its branches reach the lower abdominal wall, groin and thigh, with the femoral nerve’s sensory branch continuing down the inner leg.

Takeaway: L1–L4 form the main network; use psoas major as the central landmark.

Key terms

L1–L4 — First to fourth lumbar spinal nerve levels.

Plexus — A network of interconnected nerve branches.

Psoas major — Deep muscle running from the lower spine toward the upper thigh; helps lift the thigh.

Slide 2: Learning objectives: six nerves, three questions each

Learn the iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal, lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh, genitofemoral, femoral and obturator nerves. For each, ask: which spinal levels form it (origin), where does it travel (course), and what muscles or skin does it supply (distribution)? Then connect that supply to a clinical test. Motor fibres activate muscles; sensory fibres carry feeling. A cutaneous nerve supplies skin sensation.

Takeaway: For every nerve: roots → route → muscles/skin → test.

Key terms

Origin — Where a structure starts; here, the contributing spinal nerve levels.

Course — The route a structure follows.

Distribution — The structures supplied by a nerve.

Cutaneous — Relating to skin.

Slide 3: Read the plexus diagram from roots to branches

Start with L1–L4 on the right of the diagram and follow joined fibres toward the named nerves. T12 may contribute at the top; below, part of L4 joins L5 as the lumbosacral trunk, linking to the sacral plexus for the pelvis and lower limb. Small direct branches supply psoas and iliacus. The accessory obturator nerve is a variable extra branch, not one of the six main nerves required here.

Takeaway: Trace a named nerve back to its spinal levels; do not treat the plexus as one single cable.

Key terms

Root value — Spinal nerve levels contributing fibres to a named nerve.

T12 — Twelfth thoracic spinal nerve, just above the lumbar levels.

Lumbosacral trunk — Connection made mainly by L4 and L5 fibres toward the sacral nerve network.

Iliacus — Hip-flexing muscle lining the inside of the pelvic bone.

Slide 4: Root pattern: two nerves in each group

The slide groups the six nerves by how many main spinal levels contribute: two nerves from one level, two from two levels, and two from three levels. This is a memory framework, not six nerves all sharing identical roots. Pair the pattern with the next slide: the one-level pair is mainly L1; the two-level pairs are L1–L2 and L2–L3; the three-level pair is L2–L4.

Takeaway: Remember 2 × 1 level, 2 × 2 levels, 2 × 3 levels; then name the pairs.

Key terms

Spinal level — The numbered spinal nerve contributing fibres.

Contribution — Fibres joining a nerve from a particular spinal level.

Slide 5: The six root values: learn the pairs

Mainly L1: iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves. Two levels: genitofemoral is L1–L2; lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh is L2–L3. Three levels: femoral and obturator nerves are both L2–L4. The diagram also shows a possible T12 contribution to the upper plexus, so “one root” is the lecture’s simplified dominant-root rule. A nerve name describes its destination, not necessarily a single spinal level.

Takeaway: L1 / L1; L1–L2 / L2–L3; L2–L4 / L2–L4.

★ Know the root values of all six key nerves.
Key terms

Iliohypogastric — Nerve named for the iliac/hip region and lower abdomen.

Ilioinguinal — Nerve travelling toward the groin.

Genitofemoral — Nerve with genital and upper-thigh branches.

Slide 6: Psoas is the nerve-route map

Psoas major is the central muscle. Iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal and lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh emerge laterally (at its outer edge); the femoral nerve also emerges laterally and descends between psoas and iliacus. Genitofemoral pierces psoas and runs on its front. Obturator emerges medially (inner edge) and descends into the pelvis. Quadratus lumborum is the more lateral back-wall muscle crossed by the upper nerves.

Takeaway: Psoas: genitofemoral in front, obturator medial, the other four lateral.

★ Use the front and two edges of psoas to locate the six branches.
Key terms

Lateral / medial — Away from / toward the body’s midline.

Pierce — Pass through a structure.

Quadratus lumborum — Square-shaped deep muscle between the lowest rib and pelvic rim.

Slide 7: Full anatomy view: follow each nerve toward the groin

The numbered lower-back levels lie beside psoas major. Trace upper iliohypogastric/ilioinguinal branches across quadratus lumborum, genitofemoral on psoas, and the lateral cutaneous nerve across iliacus toward the outer groin. Femoral descends between psoas and iliacus to the front thigh; obturator follows the inner pelvic wall toward the obturator canal. The inguinal ligament is the groin band between the front hip point and pubis.

Takeaway: Same nerve routes, now placed on the muscles and pelvic bones.

Other orientation labels: pectineus is an upper inner-thigh muscle; adductor longus/brevis draw the thigh inward; sartorius crosses the front thigh; tensor fasciae latae tightens its outer connective-tissue band. The sacral plexus lies below the lumbar network.

Key terms

Inguinal — Relating to the groin.

Inguinal ligament — Band from the front hip point to a pubic attachment.

Obturator canal — Small passage at the upper part of the membrane-covered opening in the hip bone.

Slide 8: L1-derived nerves behind the kidney

The iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves leave the outer border of psoas and cross the front of quadratus lumborum below the 12th rib, heading toward the iliac crest (top pelvic rim). The diagram is a posterior view: left and right refer to the person’s sides. The kidney lies in front of this back-wall region; the diaphragm forms the roof above. These relationships matter when approaching the kidney from behind.

Takeaway: Below rib 12, the two L1 nerves cross quadratus lumborum behind the kidney.

Key terms

Posterior view — Looking at the body from behind.

Iliac crest — Top rim of the large pelvic bone.

Diaphragm — Main breathing muscle separating chest from abdomen.

Slide 9: Posterior kidney dissection: superficial to deep

The left half retains superficial back muscles; the right half removes them to reveal deeper structures and the right kidney. Ribs 10–12 orient the upper part; the 12th rib is the key lower-rib landmark. Behind the kidney are diaphragm above and psoas, quadratus lumborum and transversus abdominis below/laterally, with nerves crossing this region. Compare depth rather than assuming both halves show the same tissue layer.

Takeaway: Kidney exposure from behind crosses muscle layers before reaching the organ and nearby nerves.

Key terms

Superficial — Closer to the skin.

Deep — Further from the skin.

Transversus abdominis — Deep flat abdominal muscle whose fibres run around the trunk.

Slide 10: Vertical section: pleura, kidney coverings and nerves

Above rib 12, pleura (lung lining) forms the costodiaphragmatic recess between ribs and diaphragm; the adrenal gland sits above the kidney. From kidney outward: renal capsule → perinephric fat → renal fascia → paranephric fat. Behind these lie quadratus lumborum, the subcostal vein/artery/nerve, then iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves heading toward the iliac crest. The close pleural and nerve relationships explain why depth matters near rib 12.

Takeaway: Kidney coverings: capsule, inner fat, fascia, outer fat; nearby pleura and nerves need localisation.

Key terms

Renal — Relating to the kidney.

Costodiaphragmatic recess — Low pocket of the lung lining between chest wall and diaphragm.

Perinephric / paranephric fat — Fat inside / outside the renal fascial envelope.

Renal fascia (Gerota’s fascia) — Connective-tissue envelope around kidney and surrounding fat.

Subcostal — Below the rib; here, below rib 12.

Suprarenal / adrenal gland — Hormone-producing gland above the kidney.

Slide 11: Skin of the lower abdomen and groin

The drawing runs down through thoracic levels T7–T12 toward the lower abdominal nerves. T10 is near the umbilicus (belly button). “IH” means iliohypogastric: skin above the pubis, with a side branch toward the upper outer buttock. “II” means ilioinguinal: upper inner thigh and front genital skin. “GF” means genitofemoral: a small upper front-thigh area plus a genital branch. Neighbouring territories overlap rather than forming perfectly sharp boundaries.

Takeaway: Iliohypogastric: lower abdomen; ilioinguinal: upper inner thigh/genitals; genitofemoral: genital/upper thigh.

Key terms

Umbilicus — Belly button.

Pubis — Front part of the pelvic bone.

Sensory territory — Area of skin sending sensation through a particular nerve.

Slide 12: Inguinal canal: ilioinguinal nerve and cremaster

The ilioinguinal nerve runs in the inguinal canal, the slanting passage through the lower abdominal wall, and exits the superficial ring near the pubic tubercle. It enters through the wall, not the deep ring. The spermatic cord passes through the canal to the testis; the cremaster is its muscle covering that lifts the testis. Motor supply to cremaster is the genital branch of genitofemoral, not ilioinguinal. Femoral vessels pass below the inguinal ligament.

Takeaway: Ilioinguinal is in the canal; genitofemoral supplies cremaster movement.

★ Do not confuse a nerve travelling beside the cord with the nerve making cremaster contract.

Orientation labels: the anterior superior iliac spine is the front hip point; the deep ring supplies an inner cord covering from transversalis fascia. Rectus abdominis is the front abdominal strap; pyramidalis is a small lower midline muscle. The bladder lies behind the pubis, with prevesical fascia in front and the median umbilical ligament (urachus remnant) heading toward the belly button. External oblique aponeurosis is a broad tendon sheet over the pubic symphysis, the joint between the two pubic bones.

Key terms

Inguinal ring — An opening at an end of the groin canal.

Cremaster — Muscle that raises the testis.

Spermatic cord — Bundle containing the sperm duct, vessels and coverings going to the testis.

Pubic tubercle — Small front pelvic bump near the superficial ring.

Slide 13: Cremasteric reflex: input versus output

Stroking the upper inner thigh normally raises the testis on that side. Sensory input travels via ilioinguinal; motor output travels via the genital branch of genitofemoral to cremaster. The loop mainly uses L1–L2 spinal levels. The slide lists an absent reflex with testicular torsion (twisted spermatic cord), lower motor neurone disease and ilioinguinal injury during hernia repair. Absence is a clue; it is not a diagnosis on its own.

Takeaway: Reflex: ilioinguinal IN → L1–L2 → genitofemoral OUT → testis rises.

★ Learn the separate sensory and motor limbs of the cremasteric reflex.
Key terms

Reflex — An automatic response to a stimulus.

Afferent / efferent — Signal travelling in toward the spinal cord / out toward a muscle.

Lower motor neurone — Nerve cell carrying the final movement signal from the spinal cord to muscle.

Hernia repair — Operation to fix tissue protruding through an abnormal opening.

Slide 14: Behind the caecum: muscles, nerves and vessels

The caecum is the first pouch of the large bowel in the lower right abdomen; this drawing reveals structures behind it. Psoas major is medial and iliacus lateral. Femoral nerve lies between them; lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh crosses iliacus. The right ureter (urine tube) and testicular artery descend on/near psoas; the external iliac artery heads under the inguinal ligament to become femoral artery. These are close neighbours of bowel and appendix.

Takeaway: Behind the caecum, place psoas and iliacus first, then follow the nerves and vessels.

Key terms

Caecum — First pouch of the large intestine, usually in the lower right abdomen.

Ureter — Tube carrying urine from kidney to bladder.

External iliac artery — Main artery continuing toward the leg beneath the groin ligament.

Slide 15: Dissection key: identify all fourteen labels

Muscles: 1 psoas major; 2 psoas minor (small muscle in front of major); 3 iliacus; 4 quadratus lumborum; 5 the three lateral abdominal wall layers. Neighbours: 6 kidney; 7 ureter; 8 external iliac artery; 9 sacral promontory (forward-projecting top of the sacrum). Nerves: 10 iliohypogastric; 11 ilioinguinal; 12 lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh; 13 femoral; 14 genitofemoral. Trace the nerves relative to psoas rather than memorising numbers alone.

Takeaway: Numbers 10–14 identify five plexus branches; the obturator nerve is not numbered here.

Key terms

Psoas minor — Small, variably present muscle lying in front of psoas major.

Lateral abdominal wall layers — External oblique, internal oblique and transversus abdominis: three muscle layers around the side of the abdomen.

Sacrum — Triangular bone at the bottom of the spine forming the back of the pelvis.

Sacral promontory — Front-projecting upper edge of the sacrum.

Slide 16: Posterior abdominal wall: reconstruct the routes

The diaphragm forms the upper boundary; psoas major runs beside the spine and iliacus fills the inner pelvic bowl. Subcostal, iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves cross outward toward the abdominal wall near transversus abdominis. Lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh heads across iliacus toward the outer inguinal ligament. Femoral descends between iliacus and psoas; genitofemoral runs on psoas. The groin ligament marks the transition toward the thigh.

Takeaway: Follow muscle surfaces to predict the route of each labelled nerve.

Key terms

Posterior abdominal wall — Muscles and other structures forming the back of the abdominal cavity.

Subcostal nerve — Front branch of T12, passing below the lowest rib.

Iliacus — Muscle lining the inside of the broad pelvic bone.

Slide 17: Psoas sheath abscess: a route toward the groin

An abscess is a pocket of infection containing pus. Psoas is enclosed by a fascial sheath (connective-tissue sleeve), so infection can track along this sleeve from the lower back toward the groin and upper thigh. The drawing follows the muscle toward and below the inguinal ligament. Movement stretching the inflamed muscle can hurt; the patient may prefer a flexed hip because bending the thigh reduces that stretch.

Takeaway: A deep psoas infection can spread along its sheath toward the groin.

Key terms

Abscess — Local collection of pus caused by infection.

Sheath — A sleeve of tissue around a structure.

Hip flexion — Bringing the thigh toward the abdomen.

Slide 18: Psoas sign: why the appendix can cause movement pain

The appendix is a narrow bowel tube attached to the caecum. If an inflamed appendix lies close to psoas, stretching that muscle can trigger right lower abdominal pain. The shown test extends the right hip backwards while the person lies on the left side; this stretches psoas. Pain may also occur with resisted hip flexion because psoas contracts. A positive psoas sign supports nearby irritation but does not by itself prove appendicitis.

Takeaway: Extend hip → stretch psoas → pain if neighbouring inflammation irritates it.

★ Explain the test through the appendix–psoas relationship.
Key terms

Appendicitis — Inflammation of the appendix.

Hip extension — Moving the thigh backwards relative to the trunk.

Resisted flexion — Trying to lift the thigh while an opposing force holds it down.

Slide 19: Meralgia paraesthetica: a trapped skin nerve

The lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh, also called lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, arises from L2–L3, crosses iliacus and passes near the anterior superior iliac spine (front hip point) under the inguinal ligament. Compression there causes meralgia paraesthetica: burning, tingling or numbness over the outer/front-outer thigh. It is a sensory-only nerve, so this local lesion should not weaken quadriceps or remove the knee reflex.

Takeaway: Outer-thigh altered sensation without muscle weakness → think lateral cutaneous nerve entrapment.

★ Lateral thigh sensory symptoms with preserved strength distinguish this lesion from femoral injury.
Key terms

Meralgia paraesthetica — Altered sensation or burning in the outer thigh from irritation of its skin nerve.

Compression / entrapment — A nerve being pressed or trapped.

Anterior superior iliac spine — Prominent front point of the hip bone.

Slide 20: Lateral cutaneous nerve: branches and skin patch

The diagram follows the nerve from near psoas across iliacus, beneath/near the outer inguinal ligament and down the thigh. Anterior and posterior branches spread to the shaded outer-thigh skin area. Cutaneous means skin supply: this nerve does not drive a muscle. To assess it, compare light touch in this patch on both sides, looking for altered sensation in the distribution shown.

Takeaway: Test the outer-thigh skin patch; there is no motor action to test for this nerve.

Key terms

Anterior / posterior branch — Branch toward the front / back of the thigh.

Sensory-only — Carrying feeling without supplying muscle movement.

Light touch — Gentle contact used to compare skin sensation.

Slide 21: Femoral nerve: muscles, skin and knee reflex

Femoral nerve is L2–L4. It passes between psoas and iliacus, then below the inguinal ligament, lateral to the femoral artery and outside the femoral sheath. Branches supply iliacus, sartorius, usually pectineus and all four quadriceps heads. Skin branches cover the front/inner thigh; saphenous continues to the inner leg and medial foot border, with an infrapatellar branch below the kneecap. Test knee extension, knee-jerk reflex (L3–L4, mainly L4) and sensation.

Takeaway: Femoral nerve: knee extension + knee reflex + front thigh and saphenous skin supply.

★ Weak knee extension with a reduced knee jerk suggests femoral nerve dysfunction.
Key terms

Saphenous nerve — Sensory continuation of femoral nerve to inner leg and medial foot.

Femoral sheath — Sleeve around upper femoral vessels; the femoral nerve lies outside it.

Knee-jerk reflex — Patellar tendon tap producing quadriceps contraction and knee extension.

Quadriceps heads — Rectus femoris, vastus medialis, vastus lateralis and vastus intermedius: four front-thigh muscles.

Memory / clinical link

Diagram clarification: the saphenous area is the medial foot border near the ankle, not the whole top of the foot. The clinical tests expand the lecture’s nerve-integrity objective.

Slide 22: Obturator nerve: inner-thigh muscles and adduction

Obturator nerve is L2–L4, leaves the medial side of psoas and enters the thigh through the obturator canal. Adductor brevis separates anterior from posterior divisions. Anterior supplies longus, gracilis and usually brevis; posterior supplies obturator externus and the adductor part of magnus. Hip and knee branches explain referred pain; a small inner-thigh skin area is variable. Test adduction: squeeze the thighs together against resistance.

Takeaway: Obturator: L2–L4 → canal → inner-thigh adduction; brevis separates the divisions.

★ Femoral extends the knee; obturator adducts the thigh. Both arise from L2–L4.
Key terms

Adduction — Bringing the thigh toward the body’s midline.

Obturator externus — Deep muscle outside the obturator membrane that rotates the thigh outward.

Referred pain — Pain felt away from the structure producing it.

Genicular branch — Branch supplying the knee region.

Memory / clinical link

Diagram clarification: obturator internus is shown as a neighbouring structure, not an obturator-nerve target; it has its own sacral-plexus nerve. The hamstring part of adductor magnus is supplied by the tibial division of sciatic nerve. Brevis supply can vary.

Slide 23: Final skin map: compare named nerve territories

From top down: subcostal (T12) near the outer hip; genitofemoral and ilioinguinal at the upper thigh/groin; lateral cutaneous on outer thigh; femoral over front thigh; obturator in a small inner-thigh patch; saphenous down inner leg and medial foot. The remaining map labels are sural (outer calf/foot), superficial fibular (much of the top of the foot) and deep fibular (first toe web space). These last three are comparison territories, not extra lumbar-plexus branches.

Takeaway: These are peripheral nerve territories, not one-root dermatomes; boundaries overlap.

★ Localise sensory loss by the named nerve’s area, then combine it with motor and reflex findings.
Key terms

Dermatome — Skin area supplied mainly by a single spinal nerve level.

Peripheral nerve territory — Skin area supplied by a named nerve containing fibres from one or more levels.

Dorsum of foot — Top of the foot.

First web space — Skin between the big toe and second toe.
