Chapter 01 · Slides 4–9

One boss, two different routes

Hypothalamus, pituitary anatomy, portal circulation & hormone axes

6 slides

Slide 4 · A neuron that acts like an endocrine cell

Neuroendocrine cells are neurons that release hormonal signals into blood. Magnocellular neurons have large cell bodies and long axons ending in the posterior pituitary; their oxytocin and vasopressin (ADH) enter systemic circulation there. Parvocellular neurons have smaller bodies and shorter axons ending near the upper pituitary stalk; they release TRH, CRH, GHRH, GnRH, somatostatin and dopamine into the local portal circulation.

Posterior route: neuron → long axon → posterior pituitary → bloodstream. Hormones: ADH, oxytocin.
Anterior route: neuron → portal blood → anterior pituitary cell → its hormone → bloodstream.
💡 Why that name?

Magno means large; parvo means small. Neuro tells you that the cell is a neuron; endocrine tells you its message enters the blood. The name itself tells you the neuron size and signalling method.

Slides 5–6 · Two gland names, two anatomies

The adenohypophysis is the anterior, glandular pituitary: pars distalis, pars tuberalis and pars intermedia. The neurohypophysis is the posterior, neural portion, including the pars nervosa and infundibular stalk. The gland lies in the sphenoid bone's hypophyseal fossa; the optic chiasm lies just above it.

🧠 Make the word do the work

Adeno- means gland, neuro- means nerve, pars means part, distalis means farther away and infundibulum means funnel. Think “gland half versus neural half,” not two identical hormone factories.

Slide 7 · The hormone express lane

The hypophyseal portal circulation links two capillary beds: a primary plexus by the hypothalamus and a secondary plexus in the anterior pituitary. Hypothalamic signals travel through portal veins directly to their targets, so they arrive rapidly without first being diluted around the whole body. Fenestrated capillaries in this region permit hormone exchange and create an exception to the usual blood–brain barrier.

Hypothalamus→Primary plexus→Portal veins→Secondary plexus→Anterior pituitary
🚪 Why “portal”?

A portal circulation is a vessel link from one capillary bed to another. Picture a backstage door: hypothalamic hormones go straight to the anterior pituitary rather than taking the long public route through the whole circulation.

Chapter 02 · Slides 10–11

The pituitary's surprising origin story

One gland, two embryological starting points

2 slides

Slide 10 · A piece of mouth meets a piece of brain

An outpouching of oral ectoderm from the roof of the developing mouth grows upward to form Rathke's pouch; it eventually separates from the mouth and produces the glandular anterior pituitary. A downward extension of neural ectoderm from the developing brain forms the infundibulum and posterior pituitary.

Front = oral ectoderm
Rathke's pouch → adenohypophysis.
Back = neural ectoderm
Infundibulum → neurohypophysis.
🧩 A two-piece building

Imagine the pituitary as a house built by two teams: the mouth-roof team constructs a gland that makes hormones; the brain team constructs neural tissue that delivers hypothalamic hormones. “Rathke's” comes from the anatomist Martin Heinrich Rathke, whose name is attached to the embryonic pouch.

Slide 11 · The seam that remembers development

The pars intermedia is the region between the two lobes. Colloid-filled cysts in this region are remnants of Rathke's pouch. The lecture notes that this zone makes melanocyte-stimulating hormone (MSH) and is much less distinct in adult humans than in some other species.

Clinical bridge: developmental remnants associated with Rathke's pouch can later give rise to a craniopharyngioma (slide 18).

Chapter 03 · Slides 12–16

Meet the hormone-making cast

Histology, stains, five cell types, and how to decode the names

5 slides

Slides 12–13 · Why are there so many blood vessels?

Endocrine cells release hormones into blood, so the anterior pituitary contains clusters of hormone-producing cells closely surrounded by capillaries/blood sinuses (labelled bv on slide 13). The stain groups cells by appearance: acidophils are usually pink/red; basophils blue/purple; chromophobes stain weakly and appear pale.

🎨 The secret behind the stain names

Acido-phil means “acid-loving,” baso-phil means “base-loving,” and chromo-phobe means “colour-fearing.” These names describe staining behaviour, not which hormone the cells make. Link the colour to the cell family, then the family to the hormone.

🗝️ Translate the cell names

Cortico- = cortex (adrenal cortex); gonado- = gonads (reproductive organs); thyro- = thyroid; lacto- = milk; somato- = body. -troph evokes nourishing or supporting. A somatotroph's GH supports growth, and a lactotroph's PRL supports lactation.

Chapter 04 · Slides 17–20

When the plot goes wrong

Tumours, anatomy, transcription factors and hypopituitarism

4 slides

Slide 17 · Two ways an adenoma causes trouble

Functioning adenomas produce an excess of a hormone; non-functioning adenomas need not secrete an excess but can compress normal pituitary tissue and contribute to hypopituitarism (reduced pituitary hormone output). A growing mass can also compress nearby visual pathways, particularly the optic chiasm above the pituitary, leading to visual field problems.

Overproduction problem
Functioning tumour → excessive hormone secretion.
Space problem
Mass effect → normal gland/nearby pathway compression.
👀 Why sight and hormones can change together

Look back at the position of the optic chiasm on slide 6. The pituitary sits below it, so a tumour growing upwards can affect vision while simultaneously disturbing endocrine tissue. Hypo- means low; hyper- means high.

Slide 18 · Craniopharyngioma connects to embryology

The lecture describes a usually benign, slow-growing epithelial tumour arising from remnants associated with Rathke's pouch. Its predominantly suprasellar position can affect the hypothalamus, optic chiasm and pituitary even though it is usually benign.

Rathke's pouch→Embryological remnants→Craniopharyngioma→Possible endocrine + visual effects

Slide 19 · Cell fates depend on genetic switches

The mouse embryogenesis diagram shows transcription factors steering precursor cells into distinct endocrine lineages. PROP1 is upstream in a pathway leading to PIT1 and GATA2. The PIT1 branch gives rise to somatotrophs, lactotrophs and thyrotrophs; the GATA2 branch contributes to thyrotrophs and gonadotrophs. The separate T-pit/Tbx19 branch contributes to corticotrophs and melanotrophs.

🎭 A casting director for cells

A transcription factor controls which genes are expressed. Picture early pituitary cells as actors, and the transcription factors as directors assigning roles: make GH, PRL, TSH, FSH/LH or ACTH. A mutation in an early director affects multiple roles, not just one hormone.

Slide 20 · Two mouse models prove the point

ModelMutationReduced hormones in lectureOther findings
Ames dwarf mouseRecessive PROP1PRL, GH, TSH, LH, FSH~30% normal size; sub-/infertility

Chapter 05 · Slides 21–22

The six major messengers

Hormone abbreviations, targets and structural families

2 slides

Slide 21 · Six hormones, only five main cell types

The gonadotroph produces both FSH and LH; the remaining four main endocrine cell types produce one of the other four major hormones (ACTH, GH, PRL, TSH). The lecture describes these as peptide hormones and emphasises that prolactin is the exception to the main trophic hormones. Hypothalamic regulatory hormones bind cell-surface receptors on anterior pituitary cells to trigger hormone release.

“Trophic” is about a target: a trophic hormone supports or stimulates another gland. PRL primarily targets milk-producing mammary tissue rather than directing another endocrine gland.

Slide 22 · The three families

FamilyMembers shown in lectureRemember this
MelanocortinsACTH, LPH, MSHDerived from the common POMC precursor
SomatomammotropinsGH, PRL, placental lactogenGrowth / mammary links in the names
GlycoproteinsLH, FSH, TSHShared α subunit; distinct β subunits
🔤 Decode the initials

ACTH = adreno-cortico-tropic hormone (targets adrenal cortex). TSH = thyroid-stimulating hormone. FSH = follicle-stimulating hormone. GH = growth hormone. PRL = prolactin (pro- = promoting, lact- = milk). These names are mini flashcards: they often say what the hormone does or where it acts.

Exam shortcut: LH, FSH and TSH share their alpha subunit; their different beta subunits distinguish the hormones. ACTH, MSH and LPH share a precursor, which is a different kind of relationship.

Chapter 06 · Slides 23–28

Prolactin: the hormone with a brake pedal

Regulation, milk synthesis, suckling reflex and hyperprolactinemia

6 slides

Slide 23 · The brake is normally ON

Prolactin (PRL) is released from anterior pituitary lactotrophs, and its major physiological role is lactation. Unlike the other major pathways, hypothalamic dopamine inhibits prolactin secretion. The lecture notes that oestrogen/progesterone rise alongside PRL during pregnancy and mentions a proposed prolactin-releasing factor. It also briefly raises possible immune and nurturing roles.

🚗 A counter-intuitive memory story

Most hypothalamic releasing hormones are like an accelerator. Dopamine is a brake on prolactin. When a baby suckles, the brake is eased (less dopamine), so prolactin increases. It is disinhibition: removing inhibition is enough to increase a signal.

Slides 24–25 · Build the milk factory, then make milk

Mammogenesis is development of the functional breast. It begins around puberty in response to oestrogen and progesterone and continues during pregnancy; prolactin promotes lobuloalveolar development, especially in the latter half of pregnancy. Lactogenesis means milk production, for which prolactin is necessary before and after childbirth. The glandular alveoli contain milk-producing lactocytes.

Mammogenesis
“Mam(m)-” = breast; “-genesis” = development. Build the factory.
Lactogenesis
“Lacto-” = milk; “-genesis” = production/formation. Make the product.

Slide 26 · The suckling reflex, step by step

After childbirth, oestrogen and progesterone fall sharply; intermittent nursing-associated PRL secretion helps maintain milk production. The diagram traces: nipple mechanoreceptor input → hypothalamic reduction in dopamine secretion → less dopamine in the portal system → more anterior pituitary PRL → stimulation of breast gland cells → milk synthesis.

Suckling→↓ Dopamine→↑ Prolactin→↑ Milk synthesis
Useful extra connection (beyond these slides): prolactin supports milk production; oxytocin from the posterior pituitary supports milk ejection. Make versus move!

Slide 27 · Why breastfeeding can pause menstruation

The slide compares normal hormone patterns with hyperprolactinemia: when PRL is high, GnRH is inhibited and pituitary LH/FSH fall, disturbing gonadal function. This helps explain lactational amenorrhea (absence of menstruation during lactation).

↑ PRL→↓ GnRH→↓ LH & FSH→Reduced reproductive function
🗓️ The term says it all

A- = without, meno- = month, -rrhea = flow. “Amenorrhea” is absence of menstrual flow. The naming idea also helps with galacto-rrhea: milk flow/discharge.

Slide 28 · Too much prolactin

Hyperprolactinemia means abnormally high PRL. The lecture links it to functioning prolactin-secreting pituitary adenomas (prolactinomas) and other lesions that reduce dopamine delivery, removing inhibition. Clinical effects include galactorrhea, amenorrhea, hypogonadism, impaired fertility and reduced libido. The slide cites approximate percentages for selected groups, but these are lecture-reported figures, not universal rates for every patient group.

One cause, two outcomes: ↑ PRL can increase inappropriate milk production and suppress reproductive function via ↓ GnRH → ↓ LH/FSH.

1 · Routes & anatomy · 4–7, 9

Magno: large neurons; long axons → posterior pituitary → systemic blood; ADH + oxytocin. Parvo: smaller neurons; short axons → upper stalk → portal blood; releasing/inhibiting factors controlling anterior pituitary.

Adenohypophysis: anterior gland (pars distalis, tuberalis, intermedia). Neurohypophysis: posterior neural tissue (pars nervosa and stalk). Pituitary sits in sphenoid hypophyseal fossa; optic chiasm lies above.

Portal: primary plexus → portal veins → secondary plexus; rapid local delivery, less systemic dilution. Fenestrated capillaries allow exchange.

2 · Development · 10–11

Oral ectoderm → Rathke's pouch → anterior pituitary. Neural ectoderm → infundibulum → posterior pituitary. Pars intermedia lies between the two; colloid cysts can be pouch remnants; lecture notes MSH and lesser prominence in adult humans.

Link: Rathke's pouch remnants → craniopharyngioma (slide 18).

Chromophobes: poorly staining, described in the lecture as “retired” endocrine cells. Blood sinuses: lie next to endocrine cell clusters for hormone release.

4 · Tumours & visual links · 17–18

Functioning adenoma: hormone excess. Non-functioning: mass effect, compresses normal tissue → hypopituitarism; compression of optic pathways → visual field disturbance.

Craniopharyngioma: typically benign slow-growing epithelial tumour linked to embryological pouch remnants, mostly suprasellar; may affect hypothalamus, pituitary and vision because of location.

5 · Cell lineage & mouse examples · 19–20

Lecture's mouse differentiation diagram: PROP1 → PIT1 (somatotroph, lactotroph, thyrotroph) and GATA2 (thyrotroph, gonadotroph); T-pit/Tbx19 → corticotroph/melanotroph pathway.

Ames: recessive PROP1; low GH, PRL, TSH, LH, FSH; small/subfertile. Snell: recessive PIT1; low GH, PRL, TSH. Lecture notes ~50% increased longevity in these mice and relevance of related mutations to some human syndromes.

6 · Hormone families · 21–22

Six major: ACTH, FSH, LH, GH, PRL, TSH. Five main cell types because gonadotrophs make two.

Melanocortin family: ACTH, LPH, MSH ← common precursor POMC. Somatomammotropins: GH, PRL, placental lactogen. Glycoproteins: LH, FSH, TSH share an α subunit but have distinct β subunits.

Hypothalamic factors bind cell-surface receptors. Lecture identifies PRL as the exception to the major trophic hormones.

7 · Prolactin physiology · 23–26

Lactotroph → PRL → mammary gland. Dopamine tonically inhibits PRL. Oestrogen/progesterone rise with PRL in pregnancy. PRL supports lobuloalveolar development and milk synthesis.

Mammogenesis: functional breast development (puberty → third trimester). Lactogenesis: milk production. Postpartum: oestrogen/progesterone fall. Suckling → ↓ dopamine → ↑ PRL → continued milk synthesis.

8 · Reproductive effects & disease · 27–28

↑ PRL → ↓ GnRH → ↓ LH/FSH → impaired gonadal function. Explains lactational amenorrhea and reproductive symptoms with hyperprolactinemia.

Causes in lecture: functioning prolactinomas or decreased portal dopamine from other lesions. Features: galactorrhea, amenorrhea, hypogonadism, impaired fertility, reduced libido. Lecture-reported rates depend on population and are not universal.
